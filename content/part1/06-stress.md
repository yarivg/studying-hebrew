# Stress and syllables

Hebrew stress is the single easiest thing to get wrong and the single fastest
way to sound less foreign. English speakers put the emphasis near the front of
a word, because English usually does. Hebrew usually does the opposite.

## The default: last syllable

The technical term is *מִלְּרַע* /milra/, "from below", meaning stress on the
final syllable. It is the norm.

::: examples
שָׁלוֹם || sha-LOM, not SHA-lom
תּוֹדָה || to-DA, not TO-da
עִבְרִית || iv-RIT
מְאוֹד || me-OD
בְּבַקָּשָׁה || be-va-ka-SHA
לְהִתְרָאוֹת || le-hit-ra-OT
:::

::: rule
When you do not know where the stress falls, put it on the last syllable. You
will be right most of the time, and the error it produces is far less
noticeable than the English-style error of stressing the first.
:::

## The exception: second from last

*מִלְּעֵיל* /mil'el/, "from above", is stress on the second-to-last syllable.
It is not random; it belongs to recognisable groups of words.

**Segolate nouns.** A large family of two-syllable nouns with an e in the
second syllable, named after the *סֶגּוֹל* that usually writes it. They are all
*מִלְּעֵיל*.

::: examples
סֵפֶר || SE-fer, a book
מֶלֶךְ || ME-lekh, a king
דֶּלֶת || DE-let, a door
כֶּסֶף || KE-sef, money
בּוֹקֶר || BO-ker, morning
עֶרֶב || E-rev, evening
יֶלֶד || YE-led, a child
:::

**Words borrowed from other languages**, which keep something close to their
original stress.

::: examples
טֶלֶפוֹן || TE-le-fon
אוֹטוֹבּוּס || O-to-bus
פּוֹלִיטִיקָה || po-LI-ti-ka
:::

**Names, very often.** *דָּוִיד* is da-VID in the Bible and DA-vid in an
Israeli classroom. Both are heard.

**A handful of extremely common words**, which simply have to be learned:

::: examples
אֶתְמוֹל || ET-mol, yesterday
עַכְשָׁיו || akh-SHAV, now, which is regular
בֹּקֶר טוֹב || BO-ker TOV, with each word stressed in its own way
:::

::: warning
Stress can be the only difference between two words. *בָּאָה* /ba'A/ with the
stress at the end is "she comes"; *בָּאָה* /BA'a/ with the stress earlier is
"she came". In the present and past of a whole class of verbs, that is the
only thing your listener has to go on. This is the strongest argument for
getting the habit right early.
:::

## Where syllables break

A Hebrew syllable starts with a consonant. Always. That is why a shva in the
middle of a word is usually silence: it is marking the end of one syllable
rather than adding a vowel.

| Word | Syllables | Stress |
|---|---|---|
| *שָׁלוֹם* | sha-lom | last |
| *סְפָרִים* | sfa-rim | last |
| *מִשְׁפָּחָה* | mish-pa-kha | last |
| *מַחְבֶּרֶת* | makh-be-ret | second from last |
| *סֵפֶר* | se-fer | second from last |
| *לְהִתְרָאוֹת* | le-hit-ra-ot | last |

Notice *סְפָרִים*: the shva under the ס is silent and the word has two
syllables, not three. An English speaker's instinct is to say se-fa-RIM. That
extra vowel is the give-away.

::: tip
Say the word too fast rather than too slowly when you are unsure. Hebrew
tolerates consonant clusters at the start of a word that English does not
have: *סְפָרִים*, *כְּתוֹבֶת* /ktovet/, *שְׁמוֹנֶה* /shmone/. Rushing gets you
closer than carefully inserting a vowel that is not there.
:::

## Listening for it

The practical test is whether a native speaker has to ask you to repeat. The
two habits worth building:

1. **Default to the last syllable** on any new word.
2. **Recognise a segolate on sight.** Two syllables, an e in the second, stress
   at the front. *סֵפֶר*, *בּוֹקֶר*, *דֶּלֶת*, *כֶּסֶף*, *יֶלֶד*, *עֶרֶב*, *מֶלֶךְ*.
   There are hundreds of them and they behave as a group.

::: note
This course marks stress nowhere, in transliteration or anywhere else, and
that is on purpose. Marking it would make the transliteration a second
notation to learn, and the transliteration is meant to be thrown away as soon
as possible. Use the "hear it" button: clicking a Hebrew example plays it, and
the stress comes with it.
:::

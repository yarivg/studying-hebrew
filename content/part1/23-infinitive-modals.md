# Infinitives, wanting and having to

The present tense has four forms. The past and the future, waiting in Part II,
have ten each. The infinitive has one. It does not change for gender, it does
not change for number, it does not change for person. After fourteen chapters
of making everything agree with everything else, this is the first form in
Hebrew that asks nothing of you at all.

## Every infinitive begins with ל

::: rule
A Hebrew infinitive always starts with *לְ* or *לִ*. That ל is the same letter
as the preposition "to", doing the same job it does in "to write".
:::

What follows the ל is the root in a shape that depends on the binyan. There
are four shapes to recognise, one per binyan you have met.

| Binyan | Present | Infinitive | |
|---|---|---|---|
| pa'al | *כּוֹתֵב* | *לִכְתּוֹב* | to write |
| pi'el | *מְדַבֵּר* | *לְדַבֵּר* | to speak |
| hif'il | *מַסְבִּיר* | *לְהַסְבִּיר* | to explain |
| hitpa'el | *מִתְלַבֵּשׁ* | *לְהִתְלַבֵּשׁ* | to get dressed |

*לִכְתּוֹב* /likhtov/, *לְדַבֵּר* /ledaber/, *לְהַסְבִּיר* /lehasbir/,
*לְהִתְלַבֵּשׁ* /lehitlabesh/.

::: tip
For the last three binyanim the infinitive is the present tense with its מ
swapped out: *מְ* becomes *לְ*, *מַ* becomes *לְהַ*, *מִתְ* becomes *לְהִתְ*.
Learn the verb in the present and you get the infinitive free.
:::

Pa'al is the awkward one, because pa'al is always the awkward one. A root with
three sound letters gives *לִ* plus the root with *וֹ* in it, but the weak
roots of chapter 20 each go their own way, and those are the verbs you will
actually say.

::: examples
לִכְתּוֹב, לִסְגּוֹר, לִלְמוֹד || regular: to write, to close, to study
לִרְאוֹת || to see
לָלֶכֶת || to go
לָגוּר, לָקוּם || to live somewhere, to get up
לֶאֱכוֹל, לָדַעַת || to eat, to know
:::

There is no shortcut for those. Learn each one as a word.

## The four words that take an infinitive

::: examples
אֲנִי רוֹצֶה לִלְמוֹד עִבְרִית || I want to study Hebrew
הִיא רוֹצָה לִלְמוֹד עִבְרִית || she wants to study Hebrew
אֲנַחְנוּ צְרִיכִים לָלֶכֶת || we have to go
אַתְּ יְכוֹלָה לְדַבֵּר לְאַט? || can you speak slowly?
הֵם אוֹהֲבִים לְבַשֵּׁל || they like to cook
:::

Look at what moves and what does not. *לִלְמוֹד* /lilmod/ is identical in the
first two sentences; *רוֹצֶה* /rotze/ changes to *רוֹצָה*. The first word
carries all the agreement and the infinitive carries none.

| | m. sg. | f. sg. | m. pl. | f. pl. |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| want | *רוֹצֶה* | *רוֹצָה* | *רוֹצִים* | *רוֹצוֹת* |
| need to | *צָרִיךְ* | *צְרִיכָה* | *צְרִיכִים* | *צְרִיכוֹת* |
| can | *יָכוֹל* | *יְכוֹלָה* | *יְכוֹלִים* | *יְכוֹלוֹת* |
| like to | *אוֹהֵב* | *אוֹהֶבֶת* | *אוֹהֲבִים* | *אוֹהֲבוֹת* |

*צָרִיךְ* /tzarikh/ is "need to, must", *יָכוֹל* /yakhol/ is "can, be able
to", *אוֹהֵב* /ohev/ is "love" and, with an infinitive, "like to".

::: warning
*רוֹצֶה* and *אוֹהֵב* are real verbs in the present tense. *צָרִיךְ* and
*יָכוֹל* are not: they are adjectives that happen to translate as verbs, and
they behave like adjectives, which is why their feminine is *צְרִיכָה* and not
*צָרִיכָה*, and why *יְכוֹלָה* is built like *גְּדוֹלָה*. In the past tense you
will need *הָיָה* in front of them. Nothing about that is visible yet, but the
shapes are worth noticing now.
:::

Two of these take a plain noun as well as an infinitive: *אֲנִי רוֹצֶה קָפֶה*,
I want a coffee, and *אֲנִי צָרִיךְ עֶזְרָה*, I need help. *יָכוֹל* does not; it
only ever takes an infinitive.

To make any of them negative, put *לֹא* in front, exactly as in chapter 22:
*אֲנִי לֹא רוֹצֶה לָלֶכֶת*, I don't want to go.

## The impersonal ones

A second group takes an infinitive with no subject in front of it at all. They
are not verbs and they have one form each, forever.

| | |
|---|---|
| *אֶפְשָׁר* | it's possible, you can |
| *אִי אֶפְשָׁר* | it's impossible, you can't |
| *מוּתָּר* | it's allowed |
| *אָסוּר* | it's forbidden |
| *כְּדַאי* | it's worth it, you'd better |

::: examples
אֶפְשָׁר לְשַׁלֵּם כָּאן? || can one pay here?
אִי אֶפְשָׁר לִלְמוֹד הַכּוֹל בְּיוֹם אֶחָד || you can't learn everything in one day
אָסוּר לְעַשֵּׁן || no smoking
מוּתָּר לְדַבֵּר עִבְרִית לְאַט || it's allowed to speak Hebrew slowly
כְּדַאי לִקְנוֹת כַּרְטִיס || it's worth buying a ticket
:::

If you want to say who it applies to, hang a *לְ* with an ending on it, from
chapter 17, off the front word: *כְּדַאי לְךָ לָנוּחַ*, you'd better rest;
*אָסוּר לָנוּ לְעַשֵּׁן*, we're not allowed to smoke.

::: rule
*אֶפְשָׁר* and its group never agree with anything. *אֶפְשָׁר לְשַׁלֵּם* is
what a man, a woman and a crowd all say.
:::

::: note
English distinguishes "I can swim" from "I'm allowed to swim". Hebrew speech
blurs it: *אֲנִי יָכוֹל לִשְׂחוֹת* covers both, and *מוּתָּר לִי* is used when
the permission is the point.
:::

::: exercise Put the infinitive to work
Say "I (m.) want to eat" || *אֲנִי רוֹצֶה לֶאֱכוֹל* || the infinitive never changes
Say "she wants to eat" || *הִיא רוֹצָה לֶאֱכוֹל* || only *רוֹצֶה* moved
Say "we (f.) have to study" || *אֲנַחְנוּ צְרִיכוֹת לִלְמוֹד* || *צָרִיךְ* takes adjective endings
Make an infinitive from *מְדַבֵּר* || *לְדַבֵּר* || pi'el: swap *מְ* for *לְ*
Make an infinitive from *מַסְבִּיר* || *לְהַסְבִּיר* || hif'il: *מַ* becomes *לְהַ*
Say "it's forbidden to smoke here" || *אָסוּר לְעַשֵּׁן כָּאן* || no subject, no agreement
Say "you (f. sg.) can't go" || *אַתְּ לֹא יְכוֹלָה לָלֶכֶת* || *לֹא* in front of the modal, not the infinitive
Why is the feminine *צְרִיכָה* and not *צָרִיכָה*? || because *צָרִיךְ* is an adjective, not a verb || it takes the same endings as *גָּדוֹל*
:::

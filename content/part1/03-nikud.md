# Nikud: the vowel points

The alef-bet has no vowels in it. To write them, Hebrew uses a system of dots
and dashes placed under, inside and above the consonants, called *נִקּוּד*
/nikud/, literally "dotting". Everything in this course is written with full
nikud, and there is a switch in the top bar that takes it away when you want
to practise without it.

The good news arrives first. Modern Israeli Hebrew has **five vowel sounds**:
a, e, i, o, u. That is fewer than English, and none of them is hard for an
English speaker to make. The bad news is that the writing system has more
signs than sounds, because it was designed for a pronunciation that had length
distinctions Hebrew lost a long time ago. Several signs now say the same
thing, and you have to know them apart by shape rather than by ear.

## The five sounds

| Sound | Signs | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a | *קָמָץ* /kamatz/ ָ, *פַּתָּח* /patakh/ ַ | *יָד* /yad/ hand, *בַּיִת* /bayit/ house |
| e | *צֵירֵי* /tzere/ ֵ, *סֶגּוֹל* /segol/ ֶ | *בֵּן* /ben/ son, *סֶפֶר*-type *מֶלֶךְ* /melekh/ king |
| i | *חִירִיק* /khirik/ ִ | *עִיר* /ir/ city |
| o | *חוֹלָם* /kholam/ ֹ or וֹ | *טוֹב* /tov/ good |
| u | *שׁוּרוּק* /shuruk/ וּ, *קוּבּוּץ* /kubutz/ ֻ | *שׁוּק* /shuk/ market |

::: rule
A nikud sign is written with the letter it follows, not the letter it
precedes. *בַּ* is read "ba", never "ab". The consonant comes first, then its
vowel, always, and this is why Hebrew has almost no words beginning with a
vowel sound.
:::

## Where the signs sit

Most of them go underneath. *חוֹלָם* is the exception that goes on top, as a
dot at the upper left of its letter. Two of the signs are written with a ו of
their own: *חוֹלָם מָלֵא* is a ו with a dot above it, and *שׁוּרוּק* is a ו with a
dot in its belly.

::: examples
בָּ || ba, with a kamatz underneath
בֵּ || be, with a tzere underneath
בִּ || bi, with a khirik underneath
בּוֹ || bo, with a cholam on the vav
בּוּ || bu, with a shuruk in the vav
:::

## The signs in full

| Sign | Name | Sound | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| ָ | *קָמָץ* /kamatz/ | a | *יָם* /yam/ sea |
| ַ | *פַּתָּח* /patakh/ | a | *גַּן* /gan/ garden |
| ֵ | *צֵירֵי* /tzere/ | e | *כֵּן* /ken/ yes |
| ֶ | *סֶגּוֹל* /segol/ | e | *דֶּלֶת* /delet/ door |
| ִ | *חִירִיק* /khirik/ | i | *אִישׁ* /ish/ man |
| ֹ | *חוֹלָם חָסֵר* /kholam khaser/ | o | *רֹאשׁ* /rosh/ head |
| וֹ | *חוֹלָם מָלֵא* /kholam male/ | o | *שָׁלוֹם* /shalom/ hello |
| ֻ | *קוּבּוּץ* /kubutz/ | u | *שֻׁלְחָן* in the old spelling |
| וּ | *שׁוּרוּק* /shuruk/ | u | *שׁוּק* /shuk/ market |
| ְ | *שְׁוָוא* /shva/ | none, or a very short e | *סְפָרִים* /sfarim/ books |

### The pairs that sound the same

*קָמָץ* and *פַּתָּח* are both "a". *צֵירֵי* and *סֶגּוֹל* are both "e". *חוֹלָם*
and the two ways of writing it are all "o", and *קוּבּוּץ* and *שׁוּרוּק* are both
"u". In each pair the first sign was historically long and the second short.
Israeli Hebrew merged them.

::: warning
Because they sound identical, you cannot work out which sign to write by
listening. Do not try. You learn the spelling with the word, and for most
purposes you never need to write nikud at all. What you need is to **read**
them, and for reading, a kamatz and a patakh are the same instruction: say
"a".
:::

::: note
There is one exception, and it has its own name: *קָמָץ קָטָן* /kamatz katan/,
a kamatz that is read "o" rather than "a". It turns up in a small, learnable
set of words such as *כָּל* /kol/ all and *צָהֳרַיִים* /tzohorayim/ noon. It is
rare enough that you can meet each case as it comes.
:::

## Shva, the sign for nothing

*שְׁוָוא* is two dots stacked vertically, and it is the odd one out: it usually
marks the **absence** of a vowel. In *סְפָרִים* the ס has a shva and is read
straight into the פ: sfarim, not sefarim.

Sometimes it is pronounced as a very short e, at the start of a word or after
a long vowel. The rule for which is which is real but fiddly, and Israelis do
not apply it consistently in speech. The practical version:

::: rule
A shva at the start of a word is usually a faint e. A shva in the middle of a
word is usually silence. When in doubt, say nothing: an English speaker's
instinct is to insert a vowel, and the error that sounds foreign is the extra
vowel, not the missing one.
:::

## The chataf vowels

Four letters, the gutturals א ה ח ע, dislike a plain shva and take a
compound instead: a shva with a vowel sign beside it. These are the
*חֲטָפִים* /khatafim/.

| Sign | Name | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| ֲ | *חֲטָף פַּתָּח* /khataf patakh/ | a very short a |
| ֱ | *חֲטָף סֶגּוֹל* /khataf segol/ | a very short e |
| ֳ | *חֲטָף קָמָץ* /khataf kamatz/ | a very short o |

::: examples
אֲנִי || I, with a khataf patakh under the alef
חֲבֵרִים || friends, with a khataf patakh under the khet
אֱמֶת || truth, with a khataf segol under the alef
צָהֳרַיִים || noon, with a khataf kamatz under the hei
:::

You never have to decide to use one: they belong to the spelling of the word.
What you need is to read them as short vowels rather than as full ones.

::: tip
Read the nikud as instructions rather than as decoration. Each sign says one
of six things: a, e, i, o, u, or nothing. There are ten signs and six
instructions, so the work is remembering which sign gives which, and three
minutes with the table above covers most of it.
:::

::: exercise Read the points
What sound does ָ make? || a || it is *קָמָץ*, and *פַּתָּח* ַ says the same thing
What sound does ֵ make? || e || it is *צֵירֵי*; *סֶגּוֹל* ֶ also says e
Read *בֵּית* || bet || tzere under the ב, and the י is silent here
Read *שׁוּק* || shuk || the ו with a dot inside is *שׁוּרוּק*, u
Which sign usually marks no vowel at all? || *שְׁוָוא* ְ || two dots stacked; in the middle of a word it is silence
Why do א ה ח ע take a *חֲטָף* instead of a plain shva? || they are gutturals and resist a bare shva || the compound gives them a very short vowel to lean on
How many vowel sounds does Israeli Hebrew have? || five || a, e, i, o, u, written with about ten different signs
:::

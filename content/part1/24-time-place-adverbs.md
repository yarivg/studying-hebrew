# Time, days and where things are

This chapter is mostly vocabulary, but it is vocabulary with two pieces of
grammar hidden in it: the days of the week are numbers, and the clock is
feminine. Both follow from chapter 15, and neither is guessable from English.

## The days are counted

Hebrew does not name its days after gods or planets. It counts them.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| *יוֹם רִאשׁוֹן* | day one | Sunday |
| *יוֹם שֵׁנִי* | day two | Monday |
| *יוֹם שְׁלִישִׁי* | day three | Tuesday |
| *יוֹם רְבִיעִי* | day four | Wednesday |
| *יוֹם חֲמִישִׁי* | day five | Thursday |
| *יוֹם שִׁישִּׁי* | day six | Friday |
| *שַׁבָּת* | Shabbat | Saturday |

*יוֹם רִאשׁוֹן* /yom rishon/ is Sunday, not Monday, and this is not a quirk of
translation: the Israeli week genuinely begins on Sunday. Sunday is a full
working day, the school week runs Sunday to Friday morning, and the weekend,
*סוֹף שָׁבוּעַ* /sof shavua/, is Friday and Saturday. *שַׁבָּת* /shabat/ is the
only day with a name of its own, and everything else is numbered away from it.

::: note
In speech and in writing the days are usually shortened to the letter:
*יוֹם א׳*, *יוֹם ב׳*, *יוֹם ג׳*. A shop sign listing hours will do this and
assume you can count in letters.
:::

## Telling the time

::: rule
The hours use the feminine numbers, because *שָׁעָה* /sha'a/, hour, is
feminine. *שָׁלוֹשׁ*, never *שְׁלוֹשָׁה*.
:::

::: examples
מָה הַשָּׁעָה? || what time is it?
הַשָּׁעָה שָׁלוֹשׁ || it's three o'clock
הַשָּׁעָה שְׁתַּיִים || it's two o'clock
שָׁלוֹשׁ וָחֵצִי || half past three
שָׁלוֹשׁ וָרֶבַע || quarter past three
רֶבַע לְאַרְבַּע || quarter to four
שָׁלוֹשׁ וְעֶשְׂרִים || twenty past three
עֶשְׂרִים לְאַרְבַּע || twenty to four
:::

So *וְ* adds minutes to the hour just gone, and *לְ* counts them down to the
hour coming. *חֵצִי* /khetzi/ is a half and *רֶבַע* /reva/ is a quarter;
anything else is just a number, and *חָמֵשׁ דַּקּוֹת* /khamesh dakot/, five
minutes, may be said or left out.

To say when something happens, use *בְּ*: *הַשִּׁיעוּר בְּשָׁלוֹשׁ*, the lesson
is at three.

::: warning
Hebrew counts hours on a twelve-hour clock in speech and a twenty-four hour
clock in writing. A timetable says *18:30* and a person says *שֵׁשׁ וָחֵצִי
בָּעֶרֶב*.
:::

## The parts of the day

Each of these takes *בַּ*, the *בְּ* and *הַ* of chapter 10 fused together.

::: examples
בַּבּוֹקֶר || in the morning
בַּצָּהֳרַיִים || at midday
אַחַר הַצָּהֳרַיִים || in the afternoon
בָּעֶרֶב || in the evening
בַּלַּיְלָה || at night
:::

*בַּצָּהֳרַיִים* /batzohorayim/ carries the dual ending of chapter 9, for no
reason anyone can now reconstruct.

## When

::: examples
הַיּוֹם || today
מָחָר || tomorrow
אֶתְמוֹל || yesterday
עַכְשָׁיו || now
אַחַר כָּךְ || afterwards, later
תָּמִיד || always
לִפְעָמִים || sometimes
אַף פַּעַם לֹא || never
:::

::: warning
*אַף פַּעַם* /af pa'am/ needs *לֹא* with it, the way *אַף אֶחָד* did in
chapter 22: *אֲנִי אַף פַּעַם לֹא מְאַחֵר*, I am never late. Word for word
that is "I never not am late", and Hebrew wants both halves.
:::

Two more that sit next to these: *מוּקְדָּם* /mukdam/, early, and *מְאוּחָר*
/me'ukhar/, late.

## Where

::: examples
כָּאן, פֹּה || here
שָׁם || there
לְמַעְלָה || up, upstairs
לְמַטָּה || down, downstairs
בַּחוּץ || outside
בִּפְנִים || inside
קָרוֹב || near
רָחוֹק || far
:::

*כָּאן* /kan/ and *פֹּה* /po/ mean the same thing. *כָּאן* is very slightly
more formal; both are everywhere.

For directions, Hebrew adds a *ָה* that means "towards". You will meet the
rule properly in Part II; for now, learn the three words a taxi driver needs.

::: examples
יָמִינָה || to the right
שְׂמֹאלָה || to the left
יָשָׁר || straight ahead
:::

::: tip
*יָמִין* is the right hand and *שְׂמֹאל* is the left. The *ָה* on the end turns
a place into a direction, which is why you turn *יָמִינָה* but something sits
*מִיָּמִין*, on the right.
:::

## The months, in passing

Everyday Israeli life runs on the Gregorian calendar with the international
month names, transliterated: *יָנוּאָר*, *פֶבְּרוּאָר*, *מֶרְץ*, *אַפְּרִיל*,
*מַאי*, *יוּנִי*, *יוּלִי*, *אוֹגוּסְט*, *סֶפְּטֶמְבֶּר*, *אוֹקְטוֹבֶּר*,
*נוֹבֶמְבֶּר*, *דֵּצֶמְבֶּר*. Read them aloud and they are already familiar.

The Hebrew calendar months, *תִּשְׁרֵי* and the rest, run alongside for
holidays, birthdays and gravestones, and they come with the ordinal numbers
and the dates in Part II chapter 15.

When is the Israeli weekend? || Friday and Saturday || the week starts again on Sunday

Say "quarter to eight" || *רֶבַע לִשְׁמוֹנֶה* || *לְ* counts down to the coming hour
Say "half past nine" || *תֵּשַׁע וָחֵצִי* || *וְ* adds to the hour just gone
Say "I am never late" || *אֲנִי אַף פַּעַם לֹא מְאַחֵר* || *אַף פַּעַם* keeps its *לֹא*
Say "turn right" using one word || *יָמִינָה* || the *ָה* makes it a direction
:::

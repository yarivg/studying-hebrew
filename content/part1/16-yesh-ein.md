# Yesh and ein: there is, and having

Two small words do an enormous amount of work in Hebrew. *יֵשׁ* /yesh/ means
"there is" or "there are". *אֵין* /ein/ means "there is not" or "there are
not". Between them they cover existence, availability, and the whole of
having, which Hebrew otherwise has no way to express at all.

## Existence

::: rule
*יֵשׁ* and *אֵין* never change. Not for gender, not for number, not for
anything. One form each.
:::

::: examples
יֵשׁ מַיִם || there is water
יֵשׁ בְּעָיָה || there is a problem
יֵשׁ אֲנָשִׁים || there are people
אֵין מַיִם || there is no water
אֵין בְּעָיָה || no problem
אֵין אֲנָשִׁים || there are no people
:::

Compare that with English, which has to pick between "is" and "are", and with
the adjective, which in chapter 12 had to agree in three separate ways.
*יֵשׁ* agrees with nothing. It is the least demanding word in the language.

Turned into a question by intonation alone, exactly as in chapter 14:

::: examples
יֵשׁ מַיִם? || is there any water?
יֵשׁ קָפֶה? || is there coffee?
:::

Said on its own with enough feeling, *יֵשׁ* is also the Israeli cry of
triumph, the equivalent of "yes!" when your team scores. Context makes it
obvious.

## Having

Hebrew has no verb "to have". Not a missing one, not an irregular one: there
is no such verb, and there never was. What it has instead is *יֵשׁ* plus the
preposition *לְ*, "to", carrying an ending for the person.

::: examples
יֵשׁ לִי אָח || I have a brother
יֵשׁ לָךְ עֵט? || do you have a pen? (to a woman)
אֵין לִי זְמַן || I have no time
אֵין לוֹ כֶּסֶף || he has no money
:::

Read *יֵשׁ לִי אָח* literally and it says "there is to me a brother". That is
not a quaint gloss; it is the actual grammar.

::: rule
In *יֵשׁ לִי X*, the thing owned is the subject of the sentence and the owner
is not. Hebrew states that something exists, and says who it exists for.
:::

This matters later, when the sentence has to be put in the past or the future,
because the verb agrees with the possession and not with the possessor. That
is chapter 26. For now, notice only that the owner is wearing a preposition.

## The full set of endings

| Person | Form | |
|---|---|---|
| I | לִי /li/ | to me |
| you, m.s. | לְךָ /lekha/ | to you |
| you, f.s. | לָךְ /lakh/ | to you |
| he | לוֹ /lo/ | to him |
| she | לָהּ /la/ | to her |
| we | לָנוּ /lanu/ | to us |
| you, m.pl. | לָכֶם /lakhem/ | to you |
| you, f.pl. | לָכֶן /lakhen/ | to you |
| they, m. | לָהֶם /lahem/ | to them |
| they, f. | לָהֶן /lahen/ | to them |

::: warning
*לוֹ*, "to him", and *לֹא*, "no", sound identical. They are told apart by
spelling and by position: *לוֹ* follows *יֵשׁ* or *אֵין*, *לֹא* does not.
:::

::: note
As with the pronouns in chapter 11, *לָכֶן* and *לָהֶן* are retreating in
speech, and most Israelis use *לָכֶם* and *לָהֶם* for a group of women too.
The full set is still what you write.
:::

::: examples
יֵשׁ לָנוּ בַּיִת גָּדוֹל || we have a big house
אֵין לָהּ מַחְבֶּרֶת || she has no notebook
יֵשׁ לָהֶם שְׁנֵי יְלָדִים || they have two children
אֵין לָכֶם זְמַן || you have no time
:::

## The definite object after yesh li

When the thing you have is a specific, definite thing, the object marker
*אֶת* /et/ turns up in front of it. The full story of *אֶת* is chapter 18; the
short version is that it appears before a definite object and nowhere else.

::: examples
יֵשׁ לִי סֵפֶר || I have a book
יֵשׁ לִי אֶת הַסֵּפֶר || I have the book
יֵשׁ לִי אֶת הַסֵּפֶר הַזֶּה || I have this book
:::

::: warning
Purists object to *יֵשׁ לִי אֶת*, on the grounds that *הַסֵּפֶר* is the subject
here and a subject cannot take an object marker. They are right about the
grammar and have lost the argument entirely. Every Israeli says it. Say it
too, and know why someone might wince.
:::

## Ein as a negative

*אֵין* does double duty. It is the negative of *יֵשׁ*, and it is also how you
say that something does not exist or is not available, where English would
reach for a verb.

::: examples
אֵין מָקוֹם || there is no room
אֵין כֶּסֶף || there is no money
אֵין לִי מוּשָׂג || I have no idea
אֵין דָּבָר || never mind, literally "there is nothing"
:::

::: tip
*אֵין בְּעָיָה* and *אֵין דָּבָר* are two of the most useful phrases in
everyday Israeli Hebrew. Between them they cover most of what English does
with "no worries", "sure", "don't mention it" and "it's fine".
:::

The other negative word, *לֹא*, negates verbs and adjectives rather than
existence, and it has a chapter of its own later. Keep them apart by what
follows: *אֵין* is followed by a thing, *לֹא* by a description or an action.

::: exercise Have and have not
Say "I have a brother" || *יֵשׁ לִי אָח* || literally, there is to me a brother
Say "I have no time" || *אֵין לִי זְמַן* || *אֵין* is the negative of *יֵשׁ*
Say "she has a big house" || *יֵשׁ לָהּ בַּיִת גָּדוֹל* || the adjective still agrees with *בַּיִת*
Say "there are no people" || *אֵין אֲנָשִׁים* || no agreement on *אֵין*, ever
Ask a woman "do you have a pen?" || *יֵשׁ לָךְ עֵט?* || intonation makes it a question
Say "I have the book" || *יֵשׁ לִי אֶת הַסֵּפֶר* || definite object, so *אֶת* appears
What is the Hebrew verb "to have"? || there is none || Hebrew uses *יֵשׁ* plus *לְ* with an ending
Why does *יֵשׁ* never change form? || it is not a verb and has no subject to agree with || the thing owned is the subject
:::

# Nouns: gender and number

Every Hebrew noun is masculine or feminine. Not "usually", not "when it refers
to a person": every single one, including a table, a war and an idea. The
gender is not a label sitting quietly on the word, either. It decides the form
of the adjective beside it, the verb it governs, the pronoun that replaces it
and the number that counts it. Get the gender wrong and four other things go
wrong with it.

## Reading the gender off the ending

There is no article to tell you, the way *le* and *la* do in French. What
Hebrew gives you instead is the ending, and it is reliable enough to be worth
learning as a rule.

::: rule
A noun ending in *ָה* or in *ת* is feminine. Almost everything else is
masculine.
:::

::: examples
מִשְׁפָּחָה || a family: feminine, ends in ה
תְּמוּנָה || a picture: feminine
שָׁנָה || a year: feminine
מַחְבֶּרֶת || a notebook: feminine, ends in ת
רַכֶּבֶת || a train: feminine
חֲנוּת || a shop: feminine
סֵפֶר || a book: masculine
שׁוּלְחָן || a table: masculine
כֶּלֶב || a dog: masculine
:::

### The exceptions worth knowing

A small set of very common nouns is feminine with no ending at all. Most of
them are body parts that come in pairs, which is a memorable enough
generalisation to hang the list on.

::: examples
יָד || a hand: feminine
רֶגֶל || a leg, a foot: feminine
עַיִן || an eye: feminine
אוֹזֶן || an ear: feminine
שֵׁן || a tooth: feminine
:::

And a handful of others that simply have to be learned:

::: examples
עִיר || a city: feminine
אֶרֶץ || a country: feminine
דֶּרֶךְ || a way, a road: feminine
אֶבֶן || a stone: feminine
רוּחַ || a wind, a spirit: feminine
אֵשׁ || fire: feminine
נֶפֶשׁ || a soul: feminine
:::

::: warning
Going the other way is rarer but real. *לַיְלָה* /layla/, night, ends in *ָה*
and is masculine. So is *כִּיסֵּא* /kise/, a chair, despite the alef. When a
noun breaks the rule, this course says so in the vocabulary entry.
:::

## Making it plural

Two endings, one per gender.

::: rule
Masculine plural is *ִים*. Feminine plural is *וֹת*.
:::

| Singular | Plural | |
|---|---|---|
| *סֵפֶר* | *סְפָרִים* | books |
| *כֶּלֶב* | *כְּלָבִים* | dogs |
| *יֶלֶד* | *יְלָדִים* | children |
| *מִשְׁפָּחָה* | *מִשְׁפָּחוֹת* | families |
| *תְּמוּנָה* | *תְּמוּנוֹת* | pictures |
| *חֲנוּת* | *חֲנוּיוֹת* | shops |

Notice what happens to the vowels. *סֵפֶר* does not become *סֵפֶרִים*; it
becomes *סְפָרִים*, with the whole inside of the word reshuffled. That is the
segolate family from chapter 6 behaving as a group: they all do it, and once
you have seen five of them you can predict the sixth.

::: examples
מֶלֶךְ becomes מְלָכִים || king, kings
דֶּלֶת becomes דְּלָתוֹת || door, doors
בּוֹקֶר becomes בְּקָרִים || morning, mornings
כֶּסֶף has no plural || money
:::

## The plurals that lie

A few nouns take the ending of the other gender while keeping their own. The
word stays what it was; only the ending misleads.

| Singular | Plural | Gender |
|---|---|---|
| *שׁוּלְחָן* a table | *שׁוּלְחָנוֹת* | still masculine |
| *מָקוֹם* a place | *מְקוֹמוֹת* | still masculine |
| *חַלּוֹן* a window | *חַלּוֹנוֹת* | still masculine |
| *אִישָּׁה* a woman | *נָשִׁים* | still feminine |
| *שָׁנָה* a year | *שָׁנִים* | still feminine |
| *מִילָּה* a word | *מִילִּים* | still feminine |

::: warning
*נָשִׁים* /nashim/, women, is the one to burn in. It looks masculine, it is
feminine, and it is one of the most frequent nouns in the language. An
adjective beside it takes the feminine plural: *נָשִׁים יָפוֹת*, beautiful
women, never *נָשִׁים יָפִים*.
:::

## Wildly irregular plurals

A short list, all very common:

::: examples
אִישׁ becomes אֲנָשִׁים || a man, people
בַּיִת becomes בָּתִּים || a house, houses
בֵּן becomes בָּנִים || a son, sons
בַּת becomes בָּנוֹת || a daughter, daughters
עִיר becomes עָרִים || a city, cities
יוֹם becomes יָמִים || a day, days
:::

## The dual

Hebrew has a third number, used for things that naturally come in twos. Its
ending is *ַיִים*.

::: examples
יָדַיִים || two hands
רַגְלַיִים || two legs
עֵינַיִים || two eyes
נַעֲלַיִים || a pair of shoes
מִשְׁקָפַיִים || glasses
מִכְנָסַיִים || trousers
יוֹמַיִים || two days
שְׁבוּעַיִים || two weeks
:::

For body parts it is the ordinary plural: you do not say *יָדִים*. For the
time words it means exactly two, and three days is *שְׁלוֹשָׁה יָמִים* with the
ordinary plural. The dual is a closed set; you are never asked to form a new
one.

::: note
Every vocabulary entry in this course carries its gender, and the irregular
plurals are listed with the word. Look one up rather than guessing: the
guessing rule above is good, and it is not good enough to bet an adjective on.
:::

::: exercise Gender and number
What gender is *תְּמוּנָה*, and how do you know? || feminine, from the *ָה* ending || the plural is *תְּמוּנוֹת*
What gender is *יָד*? || feminine || a paired body part, feminine with no ending
Make *סֵפֶר* plural || *סְפָרִים* || a segolate: the vowels reshuffle, they do not just gain an ending
Make *מִשְׁפָּחָה* plural || *מִשְׁפָּחוֹת* || the ה is replaced by *וֹת*
What is the plural of *אִישָּׁה*? || *נָשִׁים* || masculine-looking ending on a feminine noun
What is the plural of *בַּיִת*? || *בָּתִּים* || one of the handful that are simply irregular
Why is *עֵינַיִים* not *עֵינִים*? || eyes come in a pair, so they take the dual *ַיִים* || the same for hands, legs, ears and shoes
Name two nouns that are feminine with no feminine ending || any two of *עִיר*, *אֶרֶץ*, *דֶּרֶךְ*, *יָד*, *רֶגֶל*, *אֶבֶן* || the paired body parts are the largest group
:::

# Reading without nikud

Nothing written for adults in Israel has nikud in it. Not the news, not a bus
timetable, not a WhatsApp message, not the label on a jar. The points exist
for dictionaries, poetry, children's books, sacred texts and language courses.
Everywhere else, Hebrew is read by people who already know what the words are.

That sounds like a wall. It is really a habit, and the habit is built by four
things: the root, the pattern, the frequent words, and the sentence around
the gap.

::: tip
Use the button in the top bar. It takes the points off every Hebrew word on
the page and puts them back. Read a chapter with them, then read it again
without, and notice that by the second pass you are not missing them. That is
the entire skill.
:::

## 1. The root does the heavy lifting

A Hebrew word is built on a root, nearly always three consonants, and the root
is exactly what survives when the vowels go. The unpointed spelling is not a
damaged word: it is the root plus whatever consonants the pattern adds, which
between them are most of the information.

Take כתב. Every word below is that root:

::: examples
כּוֹתֵב || writes
כָּתַב || wrote
מִכְתָּב || a letter
כְּתוֹבֶת || an address
כַּתָּב || a reporter
כְּתִיבָה || writing
:::

Unpointed, those are כותב, כתב, מכתב, כתובת, כתב, כתיבה. Two of them are
identical on the page, and the other four are distinguished by the letters the
pattern contributed: the מ of *מִכְתָּב*, the ו and ת of *כְּתוֹבֶת*.

## 2. The pattern tells you the vowels

Once you know the patterns, the vowels are not missing at all: they are
implied by the shape. A word beginning with מ and having three root letters
after it is a noun or a participle, and its vowels are predictable. A word of
the shape מַ.ְ.ֵ. is a present-tense *הִפְעִיל* verb. This is why Part II
spends four chapters on roots and patterns: they are not grammar for its own
sake, they are the reading system.

| Unpointed | Shape | Read as |
|---|---|---|
| מדבר | m + root דבר | *מְדַבֵּר* /medaber/, speaks |
| מסביר | m + root סבר with a yod | *מַסְבִּיר* /masbir/, explains |
| התלבש | ht + root לבש | *הִתְלַבֵּשׁ* /hitlabesh/, got dressed |
| ספריה | root ספר + the ending יה | *סִפְרִיָּיה* /sifriya/, a library |

## 3. Frequent words are recognised, not decoded

A few hundred words make up most of any Hebrew text, and you stop reading them
letter by letter almost immediately. You do the same in English: you do not
spell out "the".

| Unpointed | Word |
|---|---|
| של | *שֶׁל* of |
| את | *אֶת*, the object marker |
| זה | *זֶה* this |
| לא | *לֹא* no, not |
| יש | *יֵשׁ* there is |
| אין | *אֵין* there is not |
| עם | *עִם* with, or *עַם* a people |
| כל | *כָּל* all, every |
| אני | *אֲנִי* I |
| הוא | *הוּא* he |

That list of ten is worth reading until the shapes are instant. They are in
nearly every sentence you will ever meet.

## 4. Context closes the last gap

Where two readings survive all of the above, the sentence decides. Hebrew
readers are not resolving ambiguity consciously; the wrong reading simply does
not arrive.

| Sentence | The word ספר | Why |
|---|---|---|
| הספר על השולחן | *הַסֵּפֶר*, the book | it is on a table, and it has the definite ה |
| הוא ספר לי | *סִפֵּר*, he told | after a pronoun, in a sentence with no other verb |
| הלכתי לספר | *סַפָּר*, a barber | you go to a person, and the ל says so |

::: warning
The one place this genuinely fails is names and foreign words, where there is
no root and no pattern to lean on. An Israeli reading an unfamiliar surname
guesses too, and gets it wrong, which is why Hebrew signage often prints a
name in Latin letters alongside.
:::

## The prefixes

One more thing makes unpointed Hebrew shorter than it looks: single letters
glued to the front of a word. Learn to peel them off.

| Prefix | Means |
|---|---|
| הַ | the |
| וְ | and |
| בְּ | in, at, with |
| לְ | to, for |
| מִ | from |
| כְּ | like, as |
| שֶׁ | that, who, which |

::: examples
בַּבַּיִת || in the house: ב + ה + בית, written בבית
וְהַיֶּלֶד || and the child, written והילד
מֵהָעִיר || from the city, written מהעיר
שֶׁאֲנִי || that I, written שאני
:::

So an unpointed word starting with one of those letters may be one letter
longer than the word you are looking for. When a word will not resolve, take
the first letter off and try again.

::: rule
Reading without nikud is not decoding, it is recognition. You are not working
out the vowels of an unknown word; you are recognising a word you know from
its consonant skeleton. That means the way to get better at it is to learn
more words, not to learn more rules.
:::

::: exercise Read it bare
Read בית || *בַּיִת* /bayit/, a house || or *בֵּית* in a compound, as in בית ספר
Read שלום || *שָׁלוֹם* /shalom/ || the ו is carrying the o
What does the ב do in בבית? || it is the preposition "in", fused with the definite ה || *בַּבַּיִת*, in the house
Read ספרים || *סְפָרִים* /sfarim/, books || the י marks the i of the plural ending
What are the two things that survive when the points come off? || the root consonants and the letters the pattern adds || between them they carry most of the word
Why is a name harder to read than an ordinary word? || it has no root and no pattern to predict the vowels || which is why signs often print names in Latin letters too
Take the prefix off מהעיר and say what is left || *עִיר*, a city || מ from, ה the, so "from the city"
:::

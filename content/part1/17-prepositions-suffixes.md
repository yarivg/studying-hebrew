# Prepositions with endings

English keeps its prepositions and its pronouns apart: "to me", "with her",
"of us". Hebrew fuses them. A preposition takes a personal ending and becomes
one word, and the resulting forms are so frequent that you will meet several
of them in the first hour of real conversation.

## The one-letter prefixes

Three prepositions are single letters that attach to the front of the next
word with no space at all.

| Prefix | Meaning |
|---|---|
| בְּ /be/ | in, at |
| לְ /le/ | to, for |
| כְּ /ke/ | as, like |

::: examples
בְּבַיִת || in a house
לְבַיִת || to a house
בַּבַּיִת || in the house
לַבַּיִת || to the house
בְּתֵל אָבִיב || in Tel Aviv
:::

The fusion with the article in the last pair is chapter 10 doing its job: the
*ה* of *הַ* disappears and its vowel moves onto the prefix. There is no
written form *בְּהַבַּיִת*.

## Three that stand alone

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| שֶׁל /shel/ | of |
| עִם /im/ | with |
| אֵצֶל /etzel/ | at someone's place |

*שֶׁל* is how Hebrew says possession in speech, and it needs the noun in front
of it to be definite.

::: examples
הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁל דָּוִד || David's book
הַבַּיִת שֶׁל הַמִּשְׁפָּחָה || the family's house
קָפֶה עִם חָלָב || coffee with milk
:::

::: warning
*עִם*, with, and *אִם*, if, are pronounced identically. They are different
words with different first letters, and no amount of listening will separate
them. Only the sentence will.
:::

## The endings

One set of endings covers most prepositions in this group. Learn the set once
and it transfers.

| Person | Ending |
|---|---|
| I | *ִי* |
| you, m.s. | *ְךָ* |
| you, f.s. | *ֵךְ* |
| he | *וֹ* |
| she | *ָהּ* |
| we | *ֵנוּ* |
| you, m.pl. | *ְכֶם* |
| you, f.pl. | *ְכֶן* |
| they, m. | *ָם* |
| they, f. | *ָן* |

*אֵצֶל* takes them without a single change, which makes it the cleanest word
to learn the pattern on.

::: examples
אֶצְלִי || at my place
אֶצְלוֹ || at his place
אֶצְלָהּ || at her place
אֶצְלֵנוּ || at ours
אֶצְלָם || at theirs
:::

::: note
*אֵצֶל* has no tidy English equivalent. It means "in the domain of", so
*אֶצְלִי* is my home, my office, my desk or my country depending on what is
being discussed. *אֵצֶל הָרוֹפֵא* is at the doctor's.
:::

## The full table for lamed

*לְ* is the most common of them all, and it bends the pattern in three places:
the feminine singular, the first person plural and both third person plurals.

| Person | Form | |
|---|---|---|
| I | לִי /li/ | to me |
| you, m.s. | לְךָ /lekha/ | to you |
| you, f.s. | לָךְ /lakh/ | to you |
| he | לוֹ /lo/ | to him |
| she | לָהּ /la/ | to her |
| we | לָנוּ /lanu/ | to us |
| you, m.pl. | לָכֶם /lakhem/ | to you |
| you, f.pl. | לָכֶן /lakhen/ | to you |
| they, m. | לָהֶם /lahem/ | to them |
| they, f. | לָהֶן /lahen/ | to them |

*בְּ* runs exactly parallel: *בִּי, בְּךָ, בָּךְ, בּוֹ, בָּהּ, בָּנוּ, בָּכֶם,
בָּכֶן, בָּהֶם, בָּהֶן*.

## Shel is lamed in disguise

*שֶׁל* is historically *שֶׁ* plus *לְ*, and its endings are the *לְ* endings
with the *ל* doubled.

| Person | Form | |
|---|---|---|
| I | שֶׁלִּי /sheli/ | my, mine |
| you, m.s. | שֶׁלְּךָ /shelkha/ | your |
| you, f.s. | שֶׁלָּךְ /shelakh/ | your |
| he | שֶׁלּוֹ /shelo/ | his |
| she | שֶׁלָּהּ /shela/ | her |
| we | שֶׁלָּנוּ /shelanu/ | our |
| you, m.pl. | שֶׁלָּכֶם /shelakhem/ | your |
| you, f.pl. | שֶׁלָּכֶן /shelakhen/ | your |
| they, m. | שֶׁלָּהֶם /shelahem/ | their |
| they, f. | שֶׁלָּהֶן /shelahen/ | their |

::: rule
Possession is the definite noun followed by the *שֶׁל* form: *הַסֵּפֶר
שֶׁלִּי*, the book of mine. The article on the noun is compulsory.
:::

::: examples
הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי || my book
הַמַּחְבֶּרֶת שֶׁלָּהּ || her notebook
הַבַּיִת שֶׁלָּנוּ גָּדוֹל || our house is big
הַיְלָדִים שֶׁלָּהֶם קְטַנִּים || their children are small
:::

::: warning
*סֵפֶר שֶׁלִּי* without the *הַ* is the mistake every English speaker makes,
because English says "my book" with no article in sight. In Hebrew the noun
must be definite before *שֶׁל* can hang off it.
:::

There is a second, older way to say the same thing, by welding the ending
straight onto the noun: *סִפְרִי* for "my book". It is alive in writing and in
fixed phrases, and it is Part II.

## Im goes its own way

*עִם* does not take endings at all. It hands the job to a different stem,
*אִת*, and the result is irregular, unavoidable and extremely common.

| Person | Form | |
|---|---|---|
| I | אִתִּי /iti/ | with me |
| you, m.s. | אִתְּךָ /itkha/ | with you |
| you, f.s. | אִתָּךְ /itakh/ | with you |
| he | אִתּוֹ /ito/ | with him |
| she | אִתָּהּ /ita/ | with her |
| we | אִתָּנוּ /itanu/ | with us |
| you, m.pl. | אִתְּכֶם /itkhem/ | with you |
| you, f.pl. | אִתְּכֶן /itkhen/ | with you |
| they, m. | אִתָּם /itam/ | with them |
| they, f. | אִתָּן /itan/ | with them |

::: examples
אֲנִי אִתּוֹ || I am with him
הוּא אִתָּנוּ || he is with us
הַיְלָדִים אִתָּהּ || the children are with her
:::

::: tip
There is no *עִמִּי* in ordinary speech. If you ever see it, you are reading
poetry or a legal document. The everyday word is *אִתִּי*.
:::

::: note
A second family of prepositions takes endings that look plural even when the
meaning is singular: *עָלַי* for "on me", *עָלֶיהָ* for "on her". That set
includes *עַל*, *אֶל* and *לִפְנֵי*, and it waits until Part II. Everything in
this chapter belongs to the simpler first family.
:::

::: exercise Attach the ending
Say "to me" || *לִי* || the shortest word in the family
Say "with me" || *אִתִּי* || *עִם* changes stem to *אִת* before any ending
Say "my book" || *הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי* || the noun must carry *הַ*
Say "her notebook" || *הַמַּחְבֶּרֶת שֶׁלָּהּ* || *שֶׁל* forms follow the *לְ* forms
Say "at my place" || *אֶצְלִי* || *אֵצֶל* takes the plain endings unchanged
Say "in the house" || *בַּבַּיִת* || the prefix swallows the *ה* of the article
What is wrong with *סֵפֶר שֶׁלִּי*? || the noun needs the article: *הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי* || English drops it, Hebrew cannot
Say "our house is big" || *הַבַּיִת שֶׁלָּנוּ גָּדוֹל* || definite noun, indefinite adjective, so it is a sentence
:::

# Pronouns and the missing "is"

Hebrew has ten personal pronouns where English has seven, because the language
splits "you" and "they" by gender. It also has no present tense of "to be",
which means a Hebrew sentence can be two words long and still be complete.

## The pronouns

| | Masculine | Feminine |
|---|---|---|
| I | *אֲנִי* /ani/ | *אֲנִי* |
| you | *אַתָּה* /ata/ | *אַתְּ* /at/ |
| he, she | *הוּא* /hu/ | *הִיא* /hi/ |
| we | *אֲנַחְנוּ* /anakhnu/ | *אֲנַחְנוּ* |
| you (plural) | *אַתֶּם* /atem/ | *אַתֶּן* /aten/ |
| they | *הֵם* /hem/ | *הֵן* /hen/ |

*אֲנִי* and *אֲנַחְנוּ* are the same for everyone. Everything else in the second
and third person splits.

::: warning
*הוּא* is "he" and *הִיא* is "she". They differ by one letter, the ו against
the י, and in unpointed text they are הוא and היא, which look nearly
identical at speed. This is worth ten seconds of attention now rather than a
year of confusion later.
:::

::: note
In speech, *אַתֶּן* and *הֵן* are quietly disappearing: many Israelis use
*אַתֶּם* and *הֵם* for a mixed or even an all-female group. The Academy of the
Hebrew Language does not endorse this, and formal writing keeps all four. You
will hear the merged version constantly and should understand it; this course
teaches the full set.
:::

## Sentences with no verb

Hebrew has no present tense of "to be". No *am*, no *is*, no *are*. A sentence
that needs one in English simply does without.

::: examples
אֲנִי סְטוּדֶנְט || I am a student
הִיא רוֹפְאָה || she is a doctor
הַבַּיִת גָּדוֹל || the house is big
דָּנָה בַּבַּיִת || Dana is at home
הֵם מִיִּשְׂרָאֵל || they are from Israel
הַקָּפֶה חַם || the coffee is hot
:::

::: rule
A Hebrew present-tense sentence is subject plus whatever it is. Nothing joins
them. English speakers reach for a word to fill the gap and there is nothing
to reach for.
:::

This is why the second *הַ* from chapter 10 matters so much. Compare:

::: examples
הַבַּיִת הַגָּדוֹל || the big house, a phrase
הַבַּיִת גָּדוֹל || the house is big, a sentence
:::

In the first, both words are definite and they form one thing. In the second,
the adjective is indefinite while the noun is not, so it cannot be describing
the noun, and the only reading left is a statement about it. That contrast is
the engine of an enormous number of Hebrew sentences.

## When a pronoun steps in as "is"

With two definite nouns, dropping the verb leaves a sentence that reads
badly, so Hebrew inserts the third-person pronoun as a hinge. Grammar books
call this the *אוֹגֵד* /oged/, the copula.

::: examples
דָּנִי הוּא הַמּוֹרֶה || Dani is the teacher
תֵּל אָבִיב הִיא עִיר גְּדוֹלָה || Tel Aviv is a big city
הַבְּעָיָיה הִיא הַכֶּסֶף || the problem is the money
אֵלֶּה הֵם הַסְּפָרִים || these are the books
:::

The pronoun agrees with the subject, not with what follows it. *תֵּל אָבִיב* is
feminine, so the hinge is *הִיא*.

::: tip
You can leave the hinge out in speech and be understood. Use it when both
sides of the sentence are definite nouns, and when a sentence would otherwise
be so short it sounds unfinished. Do not use it with an adjective: *הַבַּיִת
הוּא גָּדוֹל* is not wrong, but it is heavy, and nobody talks that way.
:::

## Past and future do have a verb

The gap is only in the present. *הָיָה* /haya/, was, is a perfectly ordinary
verb, and it is compulsory.

::: examples
אֲנִי סְטוּדֶנְט || I am a student
הָיִיתִי סְטוּדֶנְט || I was a student
אֶהְיֶה סְטוּדֶנְט || I will be a student
:::

Chapter 26 gives the full set. What matters here is the asymmetry: the present
has no verb and the other tenses do, so an English speaker who has learned to
drop "is" has to remember to put "was" back.

## Introducing yourself

Everything in this chapter shows up in the first thing you will ever say about
yourself:

::: examples
אֲנִי דָּוִיד || I am David
אֲנִי מֵאַנְגְּלִיָּיה || I am from England
אֲנִי סְטוּדֶנְט || I am a student (a man speaking)
אֲנִי סְטוּדֶנְטִית || I am a student (a woman speaking)
אֲנִי לוֹמֵד עִבְרִית || I am learning Hebrew (a man)
אֲנִי לוֹמֶדֶת עִבְרִית || I am learning Hebrew (a woman)
:::

::: warning
Notice *סְטוּדֶנְט* against *סְטוּדֶנְטִית*. A noun describing a person has a
masculine and a feminine form, and you use the one that matches you, not the
one that matches the language you are translating from. There is no neutral
option. This applies to jobs, nationalities and every present-tense verb.
:::

::: exercise Say it without a verb
Say "I am a teacher" as a woman would || *אֲנִי מוֹרָה* || a man says *אֲנִי מוֹרֶה*; there is no word for "am"
Say "the book is big" || *הַסֵּפֶר גָּדוֹל* || with a second ה it would become the phrase "the big book"
What is the difference between *הוּא* and *הִיא*? || he and she || one letter apart, and identical-looking unpointed
Say "they are from Israel", about a group of men || *הֵם מִיִּשְׂרָאֵל* || a group of women would be *הֵן*
Why does *דָּנִי הוּא הַמּוֹרֶה* need the *הוּא*? || both sides are definite, so a hinge is needed || with an adjective you would leave it out
How do you say "I was a student"? || *הָיִיתִי סְטוּדֶנְט* || the past does have a verb; only the present drops it
Which two pronouns are the same for both genders? || *אֲנִי* and *אֲנַחְנוּ* || everything in the second and third person splits
Translate *הַקָּפֶה חַם* || the coffee is hot || subject, then what it is, and nothing in between
:::

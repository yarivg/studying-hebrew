# Present tense: pa'al

Here is the good news, and it is genuinely good. A Hebrew verb in the present
tense has four forms. Not six, not twelve. Four, and they do not care who is
speaking.

## Four forms, no person

English marks the person: I write, he writes. French and Spanish mark it six
ways over. Hebrew marks only gender and number, and marks the person not at
all.

::: examples
אֲנִי כּוֹתֵב || I write, I am writing (a man speaking)
אַתָּה כּוֹתֵב || you write (to a man)
הוּא כּוֹתֵב || he writes
:::

Three different subjects, one identical word: *כּוֹתֵב* /kotev/. The verb tells
you the subject is masculine and singular, and nothing else.

::: rule
The present tense has no first, second or third person. Because the verb
cannot tell you who, the pronoun is compulsory. *כּוֹתֵב* on its own is a
fragment.
:::

That last point is worth sitting with, because in Part II you will meet the
past tense, where the verb *does* carry the person and the pronoun can be
dropped. In the present it never can. Say the pronoun every time.

## The table for לִכְתּוֹב

The root is *כ-ת-ב* /k-t-v/, the idea of writing. Three letters, and the
present tense pours them into one shape.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | *כּוֹתֵב* | *כּוֹתְבִים* |
| feminine | *כּוֹתֶבֶת* | *כּוֹתְבוֹת* |

::: examples
אֲנִי כּוֹתֵב מִכְתָּב || I am writing a letter (a man)
אֲנִי כּוֹתֶבֶת מִכְתָּב || I am writing a letter (a woman)
הֵם כּוֹתְבִים בְּעִבְרִית || they write in Hebrew
הַיְלָדוֹת כּוֹתְבוֹת בַּמַּחְבֶּרֶת || the girls are writing in the notebook
:::

Note that one Hebrew form covers both "I write" and "I am writing". Hebrew has
no separate continuous tense, and no helper verb to build one with.

## The shape: o, then e

A root whose three letters all stay put through the whole conjugation is
called *שְׁלֵמִים* /shlemim/, "whole" or "sound". On a sound root the present
tense is a vowel frame you can hear.

::: sound
First root letter takes cholam: *כּוֹ*. Second takes tzere: *תֵב*. The pattern
is **o-e**: kotev, lomed, shomer, soger. Once you hear it you will recognise
a pa'al present verb in a sentence you otherwise cannot read.
:::

The feminine singular adds *ֶת* and shifts the vowel to segol: *כּוֹתֶבֶת*
/kotevet/. The two plurals push the tzere out entirely and end in the ordinary
noun endings *ִים* and *וֹת*: *כּוֹתְבִים*, *כּוֹתְבוֹת*.

::: warning
The feminine singular is *כּוֹתֶבֶת*, with the *ֶת* ending, and not *כּוֹתְבָה*.
Beginners reach for *ָה* because that is what feminine nouns and adjectives do.
For pa'al sound verbs it is wrong, and it is the single most audible beginner
mistake in the present tense.
:::

## Four more on the same shape

| Infinitive | m.sg | f.sg | m.pl | f.pl | |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| *לִלְמוֹד* | לוֹמֵד | לוֹמֶדֶת | לוֹמְדִים | לוֹמְדוֹת | learn, study |
| *לִסְגּוֹר* | סוֹגֵר | סוֹגֶרֶת | סוֹגְרִים | סוֹגְרוֹת | close |
| *לִשְׁמוֹר* | שׁוֹמֵר | שׁוֹמֶרֶת | שׁוֹמְרִים | שׁוֹמְרוֹת | guard, keep |
| *לִבְדּוֹק* | בּוֹדֵק | בּוֹדֶקֶת | בּוֹדְקִים | בּוֹדְקוֹת | check |

One frame, four roots, sixteen forms you now have for free. That is the
argument for learning Hebrew by pattern rather than by word.

::: examples
הִיא לוֹמֶדֶת עִבְרִית || she studies Hebrew
אֲנַחְנוּ סוֹגְרִים אֶת הַדֶּלֶת || we are closing the door
הוּא שׁוֹמֵר אֶת הַכֶּסֶף || he is keeping the money
הוּא בּוֹדֵק אֶת הַתַּרְגִּיל || he is checking the exercise
:::

## The same word is also a noun

A present-tense verb doubles as the noun for the person who does the action.
No ending is added, no vowel changes. *כּוֹתֵב* is "writes" and also "a
writer".

::: examples
הוּא עוֹבֵד || he works
הוּא עוֹבֵד טוֹב || he is a good worker
מוֹכֵר || sells, and a seller
שׁוֹמֵר || guards, and a guard
שׁוֹפֵט || judges, and a judge
:::

Context decides which one you are looking at, and usually it is obvious. The
usual word for an author is *סוֹפֵר* /sofer/, from a different root, so do not
expect every such pair to be the word you would have guessed.

The same forms are also used as adjectives. That is where *מְעַנְיֵין*
/me'anyein/ in chapter 12 came from: it is a present-tense verb meaning "it
interests", parked in the slot where English puts "interesting". This is why
that chapter's third group of adjectives took *ֶת* in the feminine.

## How verbs are listed

::: rule
The dictionary form of a Hebrew verb is the infinitive: *לִכְתּוֹב* /likhtov/,
"to write". This course lists every verb that way, with its four present-tense
forms beside it.
:::

The infinitive always begins with *ל*. It is not the form you build the
present tense from, though: build the present from the root, using the o-e
frame above.

::: tip
To get from an infinitive to the present, throw away the *לִ* and all the
vowels, keep the three consonants, and pour them into o-e. *לִסְגּוֹר* gives
*ס-ג-ר* gives *סוֹגֵר*.
:::

Say "she is writing" || *הִיא כּוֹתֶבֶת* || the pronoun cannot be dropped
Say "we study Hebrew" (a group of women) || *אֲנַחְנוּ לוֹמְדוֹת עִבְרִית* || the verb agrees with the speakers, not the language
Why is *כּוֹתֵב* not a sentence? || the present tense has no person, so it needs a pronoun || *אֲנִי כּוֹתֵב*, *אַתָּה כּוֹתֵב* and *הוּא כּוֹתֵב* are the same word

:::

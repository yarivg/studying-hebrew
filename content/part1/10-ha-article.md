# The definite article ה

English has "a" and "the". Hebrew has only "the", and it is one letter glued
to the front of the word: *הַ* /ha/.

::: examples
בַּיִת || a house
הַבַּיִת || the house
סֵפֶר || a book
הַסֵּפֶר || the book
יְלָדִים || children
הַיְלָדִים || the children
:::

::: rule
There is no indefinite article in Hebrew. A bare noun is "a noun". To make it
definite, prefix *הַ*, and nothing else changes: not the gender, not the
number, not the word itself.
:::

That is the whole of it, and it is a genuine gift after French or German. *הַ*
is *הַ* for a masculine singular, a feminine plural and everything between.

## What the dagesh is doing

Look again at *הַסֵּפֶר*. The ס has picked up a dot. The definite article
historically doubled the following consonant, and the dagesh is the record of
it. Israeli Hebrew does not pronounce the doubling, so you can read straight
past it, but you will see it on nearly every definite noun in pointed text.

::: examples
הַבַּיִת || the house: dagesh in the ב, which it had anyway
הַסֵּפֶר || the book: dagesh in the ס
הַכֶּלֶב || the dog: dagesh in the כ, so it is "hakelev" not "hakhelev"
הַמִּשְׁפָּחָה || the family: dagesh in the מ
:::

::: warning
On בּ כּ פּ the dagesh is audible, so the article can change how the word
sounds. *כֶּלֶב* is already "kelev", but consider *פַּעַם* /pa'am/ and a word
like *בַּיִת*: they keep their hard sound. The rule bites the other way with
words that start soft, which is rare. Read what is written.
:::

The gutturals א ה ח ע and the letter ר refuse a dagesh. Instead the article
lengthens its own vowel, which in pointed text means the *ַ* becomes *ָ*.

::: examples
הָאִישׁ || the man
הָעִיר || the city
הָרְחוֹב || the street
הָהָר || the mountain
:::

And before an unstressed *ָה* or *ָע*, the article becomes *הֶ*:

::: examples
הֶחָבֵר || the friend
הֶהָרִים || the mountains
:::

::: note
These vowel changes are worth recognising, not producing. When you write, *הַ*
is right often enough that nobody will misunderstand you. When you read, you
need to know that *הָ* and *הֶ* are the same article, so you do not go looking
for a different word.
:::

## When ב, ל and כ meet the article

This is the part that actually matters day to day. Three prepositions are also
single letters glued to the front: *בְּ* in, *לְ* to, *כְּ* like. When one of
them lands on a word that already has *הַ*, the two fuse. The ה disappears and
its vowel moves onto the preposition.

| Preposition | Plus the article | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *בְּ* in | *בַּ* | *בַּבַּיִת* in the house |
| *לְ* to | *לַ* | *לַבַּיִת* to the house |
| *כְּ* like | *כַּ* | *כַּבַּיִת* like the house |

::: examples
אֲנִי בְּבַיִת || I am in a house
אֲנִי בַּבַּיִת || I am in the house
הוּא הוֹלֵךְ לְבֵית סֵפֶר || he is going to a school
הוּא הוֹלֵךְ לְבֵית הַסֵּפֶר || he is going to the school
:::

::: rule
*בְּ* plus *הַ* is *בַּ*, not *בְּהַ*. The ה is swallowed. The same for *לְ* and
*כְּ*. This fusion is compulsory: writing *בְּהַבַּיִת* is not a stylistic choice,
it is wrong.
:::

The preposition *מִן* /min/, from, behaves differently: it usually keeps the
article visible, contracting to *מֵ* before it.

::: examples
מֵהַבַּיִת || from the house
מֵהָעִיר || from the city
:::

::: tip
Unpointed, *בבית* is ambiguous: it could be "in a house" or "in the house".
Israelis read it from context and are almost never confused. When you write,
the fused form is spelled the same either way, so this is one ambiguity you do
not have to manage.
:::

## Definiteness spreads

Once a noun is definite, everything attached to it must be too. An adjective
after a definite noun takes its own *הַ*:

::: examples
בַּיִת גָּדוֹל || a big house
הַבַּיִת הַגָּדוֹל || the big house
יַלְדָּה קְטַנָּה || a small girl
הַיַּלְדָּה הַקְּטַנָּה || the small girl
:::

::: warning
*הַבַּיִת גָּדוֹל* with only one ה is not "the big house". It is a complete
sentence: "the house is big". One letter is the difference between a phrase
and a statement, and this is the single most common mistake beginners make in
Hebrew. Chapter 12 works through it.
:::

## Names and things already definite

A proper name is definite by nature and never takes the article. So does a
noun with a possessive attached.

::: examples
דָּנָה || Dana, never *הַדָּנָה*
יִשְׂרָאֵל || Israel
תֵּל אָבִיב || Tel Aviv
הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי || my book: the noun keeps its ה, the possessive adds nothing
:::

::: note
Country and city names go without the article, with a few frozen exceptions
that carry it as part of the name: *הָאָרֶץ* /ha'aretz/, the Land, meaning
Israel, and *הַנֶּגֶב* /hanegev/, the Negev.
:::

Make *סֵפֶר* definite || *הַסֵּפֶר* || the ס takes a dagesh; the word is unchanged otherwise
Make *עִיר* definite || *הָעִיר* || ע refuses a dagesh, so the article lengthens its vowel
Say "in the house" || *בַּבַּיִת* || *בְּ* plus *הַ* fuses to *בַּ*
Say "to the school" || *לְבֵית הַסֵּפֶר* || in a compound the article goes on the second noun; chapter 25 explains why
What is the Hebrew for "a house"? || *בַּיִת* || there is no indefinite article: the bare noun is enough
What is the difference between *הַבַּיִת הַגָּדוֹל* and *הַבַּיִת גָּדוֹל*? || the big house, and the house is big || the second ה is what makes it a phrase rather than a sentence
Say "from the city" || *מֵהָעִיר* || *מִן* contracts to *מֵ* and the ה stays visible
Why is there no *הַ* on *תֵּל אָבִיב*? || a proper name is already definite || the same for people's names
:::

# Final letters, print and handwriting

Five Hebrew letters are written differently when they come at the end of a
word. They are called *אוֹתִיּוֹת סוֹפִיּוֹת* /otiyot sofiyot/, final letters, and
they are not extra letters: they are the same five, wearing a different shape.

| Ordinary | Final | Name | Sound |
|---|---|---|---|
| כ | ך | *כַּף סוֹפִית* /kaf sofit/ | k or kh |
| מ | ם | *מֵם סוֹפִית* /mem sofit/ | m |
| נ | ן | *נוּן סוֹפִית* /nun sofit/ | n |
| פ | ף | *פֵּא סוֹפִית* /pei sofit/ | p or f |
| צ | ץ | *צָדִי סוֹפִית* /tzadi sofit/ | ts |

There is a mnemonic for the set that every Israeli schoolchild learns:
*כַּמְנַפֵּץ* /kamnapetz/, a made-up word holding all five in order.

## What they do

Four of the five drop a tail below the line. That is the whole point. Hebrew
is written without spaces between words far less often than you might fear,
but in a dense line of square letters the eye needs help finding where one
word stops, and a letter that hangs below the baseline is a full stop you can
see from across the page.

::: examples
מֶלֶךְ || a king, ending in ך
שָׁלוֹם || hello, ending in ם
נָכוֹן || correct, ending in ן
סוֹף || an end, ending in ף
עֵץ || a tree, ending in ץ
:::

Compare the same letters in the middle of a word:

::: examples
מַלְכָּה || a queen, with the כ in the middle
מַיִם || water, with the מ at the start
נָשִׁים || women, with the נ at the start
מִשְׁפָּחָה || a family, with the פ in the middle
מִצְוָוה || a commandment, with the צ in the middle
:::

::: rule
A final form appears only as the last letter of a word. Never at the start,
never in the middle, and never before a suffix that is written as part of the
same word. When a letter stops being last, it goes back to its ordinary shape.
:::

That last clause is the one that catches people. *כֶּלֶב* /kelev/ is a dog; the
plural is *כְּלָבִים* /klavim/, and the ב is no longer final because *ים* was
added after it. Hebrew does not spell a word one way and then keep the old
shape out of habit.

::: warning
Writing כ where ך belongs, or ם where מ belongs, is the commonest spelling
slip an English speaker makes in Hebrew, because English has nothing like it
and the sound is identical. This course marks it as **correct with a spelling
slip**: you get the mark, and you get shown the proper spelling. It is worth
fixing, but it is not worth losing the answer over.
:::

## Two that are not what they look like

ן and ו are easy to confuse: both are a single vertical stroke. The difference
is that final *נוּן* drops below the line and *וָו* sits on it. In print the gap
is obvious once you know to look; in handwriting it is the main thing keeping
them apart.

ף and ך are also close. *פֵּא סוֹפִית* has a hook at the top that curls to the
left before the descender; *כַּף סוֹפִית* is a smooth curve with no hook.

## Handwriting

Everything above is print, *דְּפוּס* /dfus/. Israelis do not write like that by
hand. Handwritten Hebrew, *כְּתָב* /ktav/, is a separate set of shapes, and some
of them look nothing like their printed counterparts: handwritten א is a
shape like a backwards N, handwritten ש is a curl, handwritten ל rises with a
loop.

This course teaches you to read print, because that is what signs, books,
screens and this page are set in. You will need handwriting the first time
someone writes you an address on a piece of paper, and the honest advice is to
learn it separately, from a chart, when that day comes. Two things to know
now:

- **Handwriting has the same five final letters**, in the same places. The
  system does not change.
- **Nobody handwrites the nikud.** The points are a printing convention, used
  in dictionaries, children's books, poetry and courses like this one. An
  Israeli adult writing a note writes bare consonants.

::: tip
If you want the handwritten shapes early, the one that pays off first is ש,
because it appears in an enormous number of words and is unrecognisable from
its printed form. The rest can wait.
:::

## Reading practice

Each of these ends in a final letter. Say the word, then say which letter is
final and what its ordinary shape would be.

::: examples
מַיִם || water
עַכְשָׁיו || now
בֵּן || a son
כֶּסֶף || money
אֶרֶץ || a country, a land
צָרִיךְ || needs to, must
יַיִן || wine
אַף || a nose
:::

::: note
*עַכְשָׁיו* ends in ו, which is not a final form: *וָו* has only one shape. Only
those five letters change, and the list does not grow.
:::

::: exercise Final or not
Write the plural of *סֵפֶר* and say what happens to the ר || *סְפָרִים* || nothing: ר has no final form, and it is no longer last anyway
Why is it *מֶלֶךְ* but *מַלְכָּה*? || the כ is last in the first word and not in the second || a letter wears its final shape only while it is last
Which of these is wrong: *שָׁלוֹם*, *שָׁלוֹמ*? || the second || a מ at the end of a word must be ם
Name the five final letters in order || ך ם ן ף ץ || the mnemonic is *כַּמְנַפֵּץ*
Is ו a final letter? || no || it looks like ן but never changes shape
Spell *אֶרֶץ* and say which letter is final || א ר ץ, the צ is final || in the middle it would be צ, as in *מִצְוָוה*
:::

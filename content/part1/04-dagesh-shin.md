# Dagesh, shin and sin

Three of the dots in Hebrew writing are not vowels. They sit inside or above a
consonant and change which consonant it is. There are only three things to
learn here, and two of them matter every day.

## The dagesh

A *דָּגֵשׁ* /dagesh/ is a dot in the middle of a letter. In six letters it
historically changed the sound; in modern Israeli Hebrew, three of those six
still do.

| Without | With | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| ב | בּ | v becomes b |
| כ | כּ | kh becomes k |
| פ | פּ | f becomes p |

::: examples
בַּיִת || a house: the ב has a dagesh, so it is "bayit"
אָבִיב || spring: the second ב has none, so it is "aviv"
כֶּסֶף || money: dagesh in the כ, so "kesef"
מֶלֶךְ || a king: no dagesh, so the final ך is "kh", "melekh"
פֶּה || a mouth: dagesh, so "pe"
סוֹף || an end: no dagesh, so "sof"
:::

::: rule
בּ כּ פּ with a dagesh are the hard sounds b, k, p. Without it they are the
soft sounds v, kh, f. Nothing else in the word tells you which; the dot is the
whole signal.
:::

The other three letters of the traditional six are ג, ד and ת. They take a
dagesh too, and in Israeli Hebrew it makes no audible difference at all:
*דָּג* and a hypothetical דָג would both be "dag". You will see the dot on
them constantly in this course, because the pointing is written properly, and
you can ignore it when reading aloud.

::: note
The full traditional set is remembered as *בְּגַ"ד כְּפַ"ת* /begadkefat/, a
nonsense word holding ב ג ד כ פ ת. Hebrew grammar books refer to it
constantly. Three of the six are now silent partners.
:::

### The other dagesh

Confusingly, the same dot does a second job: it doubles a consonant. In
*סַבָּא* /saba/ the ב has a dagesh which is not making it "b instead of v" (it
would be b anyway after that vowel) but marking that the letter was once said
twice. Grammarians call the first kind *דָּגֵשׁ קַל* /dagesh kal/ and the second
*דָּגֵשׁ חָזָק* /dagesh khazak/.

Israeli Hebrew does not pronounce doubled consonants, so the distinction is
invisible in speech. It matters for one practical reason: a dagesh khazak
tells you a letter has been swallowed, which is how you spot a verb pattern.
*מְדַבֵּר* /medaber/ has a dagesh in the ב that marks it as *פִּיעֵל* /piel/, one
of the seven verb patterns. That pays off in Part II.

## Shin and sin

The letter ש has a dot above it, on one side or the other, and the side
decides which letter it is.

| Letter | Name | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| שׁ | *שִׁין* /shin/, dot on the right | sh |
| שׂ | *שִׂין* /sin/, dot on the left | s |

::: examples
שָׁלוֹם || hello: dot on the right, "shalom"
שִׂמְחָה || joy: dot on the left, "simkha"
שֶׁמֶשׁ || sun: both dots on the right, "shemesh"
שָׂפָה || a language, a lip: dot on the left, "safa"
:::

::: tip
The dot is on the side it points to in the Hebrew: *שִׁין* has its dot on the
**right**, which is the side a Hebrew word starts from. Some people remember
it the other way: sin sounds like samekh, and *סָמֶךְ* comes before *שִׁין* in
the alef-bet, so sin gets the left, the "earlier" side for a right-to-left
reader.
:::

*שִׂין* is much rarer than *שִׁין*. When you meet a ש with no dot at all, which
happens in unpointed text every single time, assume "sh" and you will be right
far more often than not.

## When vav is a consonant

The letter ו appears in three jobs, and they look almost identical.

| Written | Job | Sound |
|---|---|---|
| ו | consonant | v |
| וֹ | *חוֹלָם מָלֵא*, a vowel | o |
| וּ | *שׁוּרוּק*, a vowel | u |

::: examples
וְ || and: a plain vav, said "ve"
עוֹד || more, still: vav with a dot above, said "od"
שׁוּק || market: vav with a dot inside, said "shuk"
וָו || the name of the letter itself: consonant, vowel-less, consonant
:::

When a word is written without nikud, all three look like a bare ו, and you
work out from the word which one it is. That is the subject of chapter 5, and
it is far less painful than it sounds.

::: warning
Two consonant vavs in a row are written וו in modern full spelling: *מִצְוָוה*
/mitzva/, *אֲוִויר* /avir/. One vav there would read as a vowel. This is a
spelling convention you will see everywhere in this course.
:::

# Greetings and the first exchange

Seven chapters of letters and sounds, and you have not yet said anything. This
chapter is the fastest possible return: about twenty-five expressions that
open and close a conversation, ask how someone is, and get you politely out of
trouble. None of it needs grammar, because all of it is fixed.

## Hello and goodbye

*שָׁלוֹם* /shalom/ means peace, and it is hello and goodbye both. It is always
correct and never warm. Israelis use it with strangers, on the phone, and in
writing far more than between friends.

::: examples
שָׁלוֹם || hello, or goodbye
הַיי || hi, borrowed straight from English and extremely common
לְהִתְרָאוֹת || see you, literally "to see one another"
בַּיי || bye, also borrowed
:::

By time of day:

::: examples
בּוֹקֶר טוֹב || good morning
צָהֳרַיִים טוֹבִים || good afternoon, literally "good noons"
עֶרֶב טוֹב || good evening
לַיְלָה טוֹב || good night, said on leaving or going to bed
:::

::: note
*צָהֳרַיִים* is plural in form, which is why its adjective is *טוֹבִים* and not
*טוֹב*. Hebrew makes several time words plural like this. It is not something
to work out now; it is a phrase to say whole.
:::

## How are you

Hebrew asks this in a form that changes depending on who you are talking to.
There is no neutral "you" in Hebrew: every word aimed at a person is either
masculine or feminine. This is the first place it bites, and it will keep
biting until chapter 11 explains it properly.

| To a man | To a woman | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| *מַה שְׁלוֹמְךָ* /ma shlomkha/ | *מַה שְׁלוֹמֵךְ* /ma shlomekh/ | how are you |
| *מַה נִשְׁמָע* /ma nishma/ | *מַה נִשְׁמָע* | how's it going |
| *מַה קוֹרֶה* /ma kore/ | *מַה קוֹרֶה* | what's up |

::: tip
*מַה נִשְׁמָע* is the one to learn first. It is the everyday version, it is the
same for everyone, and nobody will correct you for using it. *מַה שְׁלוֹמְךָ* is
more formal and has the gendered ending you are about to spend a chapter on.
:::

And the answers:

::: examples
בְּסֵדֶר || fine, okay
הַכֹּל בְּסֵדֶר || everything's fine
טוֹב, תּוֹדָה || good, thanks
בְּסֵדֶר גָּמוּר || absolutely fine
לֹא רַע || not bad
כָּכָה כָּכָה || so-so
:::

## Please and thank you

::: examples
תּוֹדָה || thank you
תּוֹדָה רַבָּה || thank you very much
בְּבַקָּשָׁה || please; here you are; you're welcome
סְלִיחָה || excuse me, sorry
אֵין בְּעָד מָה || don't mention it
:::

*בְּבַקָּשָׁה* does three jobs. It is "please" when you ask for something, "here
you are" when you hand something over, and "you're welcome" after thanks. One
word covers all three, and you will hear it forty times a day.

::: warning
*סְלִיחָה* is both "sorry" and "excuse me". Use it to apologise, to get past
someone on a bus, and to get a waiter's attention. What it is not is a way to
ask someone to repeat themselves: for that, say *מָה* /ma/, or more politely
*סְלִיחָה, לֹא שָׁמַעְתִּי* /slikha, lo shamati/, sorry, I did not hear.
:::

## Introducing yourself

::: examples
אֵיךְ קוֹרְאִים לְךָ? || what is your name (to a man), literally "how do they call you"
אֵיךְ קוֹרְאִים לָךְ? || the same, to a woman
קוֹרְאִים לִי דָּנָה || my name is Dana, literally "they call me Dana"
נָעִים מְאוֹד || pleased to meet you, literally "very pleasant"
מֵאַיִן אַתָּה? || where are you from (to a man)
אֲנִי מֵאַנְגְּלִיָּיה || I am from England
:::

*נָעִים מְאוֹד* is the standard answer to being introduced, and it is worth
practising for the stress: na-IM me-OD, both words on the last syllable.

## Useful when you are stuck

These are the sentences that keep a conversation alive when you have run out
of Hebrew.

::: examples
אֲנִי לֹא מֵבִין || I don't understand (a man speaking)
אֲנִי לֹא מְבִינָה || I don't understand (a woman speaking)
אַתָּה יָכוֹל לַחְזוֹר? || can you repeat that (to a man)
לְאַט, בְּבַקָּשָׁה || slowly, please
אֵיךְ אוֹמְרִים בְּעִבְרִית...? || how do you say in Hebrew...
מָה זֶה? || what is this
אֲנִי לוֹמֵד עִבְרִית || I am learning Hebrew (a man speaking)
אֲנִי לוֹמֶדֶת עִבְרִית || I am learning Hebrew (a woman speaking)
:::

::: rule
Notice the pattern already: *מֵבִין* and *מְבִינָה*, *לוֹמֵד* and *לוֹמֶדֶת*. A
Hebrew verb in the present tense agrees with the gender of whoever is doing
it, including when that is you. There is no way to speak Hebrew about yourself
without stating your gender. Chapter 19 makes this systematic.
:::

## Two you will hear constantly

*יַאלְלָה* /yalla/, from Arabic, means "come on" or "let's go" and is used
by everybody in Israel all the time. *בְּעֶזְרַת הַשֵּׁם* /be'ezrat hashem/,
"God willing", is used by religious speakers in the way English uses
"hopefully", and by secular speakers ironically.

::: exercise Say it
How do you greet someone at nine in the morning? || *בּוֹקֶר טוֹב* || and *עֶרֶב טוֹב* in the evening
Ask a woman how she is, using the everyday form || *מַה נִשְׁמָע* || it is the same for everyone; *מַה שְׁלוֹמֵךְ* is the formal feminine
How do you say thank you very much? || *תּוֹדָה רַבָּה* || to-DA ra-BA
What three things does *בְּבַקָּשָׁה* mean? || please, here you are, you're welcome || one word for all three
Say "pleased to meet you" || *נָעִים מְאוֹד* || na-IM me-OD, both stressed at the end
A woman says she does not understand. What does she say? || *אֲנִי לֹא מְבִינָה* || a man would say *אֲנִי לֹא מֵבִין*
What does *לְהִתְרָאוֹת* literally mean? || to see one another || it is the ordinary way to say goodbye
Ask someone to speak slowly || *לְאַט, בְּבַקָּשָׁה* || and *אַתָּה יָכוֹל לַחְזוֹר?* to ask a man to repeat
:::

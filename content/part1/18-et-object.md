# Et: the object marker

*אֶת* /et/ is a word with no meaning. It cannot be translated, it adds
nothing to the sense of the sentence, and leaving it out changes nothing
except that the sentence becomes wrong. Its entire job is to stand in front of
a direct object and announce that the object is definite.

::: rule
*אֶת* appears before a direct object, and only when that object is definite.
Never before an indefinite one.
:::

## What counts as definite

Three things make a noun definite in Hebrew, and all three trigger *אֶת*.

| The object is | Example |
|---|---|
| carrying the article *הַ* | אֶת הַסֵּפֶר |
| a proper name | אֶת דָּוִד |
| a noun with a possessive | אֶת הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי |

::: examples
אֶת הַסֵּפֶר || the book, as an object
אֶת הַמַּחְבֶּרֶת || the notebook, as an object
אֶת הַיְלָדִים הַקְּטַנִּים || the small children, as an object
אֶת דָּוִד || David, as an object
אֶת תֵּל אָבִיב || Tel Aviv, as an object
סֵפֶר || a book, as an object: no אֶת
יְלָדִים קְטַנִּים || small children, as an object: no אֶת
:::

The verb that goes in front of these is chapter 19, so the examples above are
object phrases rather than whole sentences. The one complete frame you already
have is *יֵשׁ לִי*, from chapter 16: *יֵשׁ לִי סֵפֶר* against *יֵשׁ לִי אֶת
הַסֵּפֶר*.

::: tip
The test in English: put the object into an English sentence and ask whether
you would say "the", or a name, or "my", "his", "David's". If yes, Hebrew
wants *אֶת*. If you would say "a" or nothing, it does not.
:::

A proper name and a possessed noun are already definite even though neither
one is wearing a *הַ*. This catches people out, because the *ה* is the visible
signal and it is not there. *דָּוִד* is a specific person; *הַתִּיק שֶׁלִּי* is
one specific bag. Both take *אֶת*.

## Et and the article

*אֶת* and *הַ* are written as two separate words, always, with a space between
them. In speech they run together into one unit that sounds like "et-ha",
stressed on the article.

::: sound
In fast, casual speech *אֶת הַ* collapses further, down to a single *ת* sound
glued to the front of the noun. You will hear ת'בית for *אֶת הַבַּיִת*. This is
speech only; it is never written except to represent speech.
:::

## When there is no et

An indefinite object gets nothing. There is no indefinite equivalent of *אֶת*
and no gap where one ought to be.

::: examples
יֵשׁ לִי מַחְבֶּרֶת || I have a notebook
יֵשׁ לִי אֶת הַמַּחְבֶּרֶת || I have the notebook
יֵשׁ לָנוּ בַּיִת || we have a house
יֵשׁ לָנוּ אֶת הַבַּיִת הַזֶּה || we have this house
:::

Note the last pair. *הַזֶּה* from chapter 13 only ever attaches to a definite
noun, so anything with *הַזֶּה* or *הַזֹּאת* on it automatically takes *אֶת*
when it is an object.

::: warning
Two mistakes, and English speakers make both. The first is dropping *אֶת*
before a definite object, because English has nothing there to remind you. The
second is over-correcting and putting *אֶת* before every object, definite or
not. The second one is worse: it does not sound foreign so much as garbled,
because *אֶת* is a signal that something specific is coming, and then nothing
specific arrives.
:::

## Et with a pronoun

When the object is "me", "him", "them", *אֶת* takes a personal ending, and the
stem stretches to *אוֹת*.

| Person | Form | |
|---|---|---|
| me | אוֹתִי /oti/ | me |
| you, m.s. | אוֹתְךָ /otkha/ | you |
| you, f.s. | אוֹתָךְ /otakh/ | you |
| him | אוֹתוֹ /oto/ | him, it |
| her | אוֹתָהּ /ota/ | her, it |
| us | אוֹתָנוּ /otanu/ | us |
| you, m.pl. | אֶתְכֶם /etkhem/ | you |
| you, f.pl. | אֶתְכֶן /etkhen/ | you |
| them, m. | אוֹתָם /otam/ | them |
| them, f. | אוֹתָן /otan/ | them |

Eight of the ten use *אוֹת*. The two plural "you" forms keep the short stem
and stay *אֶתְכֶם* and *אֶתְכֶן*, which is irregular and has to be noticed.
*אוֹתְכֶם* is heard in speech and is not the written form.

::: rule
A pronoun object is always definite, so it always appears in this shape. There
is no version of "I see her" in Hebrew that does not contain *אוֹתָהּ*.
:::

::: note
*אוֹתוֹ* has a second life as an adjective meaning "the same": *אוֹתוֹ דָּבָר*
is "the same thing", *אוֹתָהּ מַחְבֶּרֶת* is "the same notebook". It sits in
front of the noun there, which is the clue that it is not doing object work.
:::

## Why Hebrew bothers

Hebrew word order is freer than English word order, and there is no case
marking on nouns. *אֶת* is the one signal that says "this noun is the thing
being acted on, not the thing doing the acting". It earns its keep in Part II,
where an object can move to the front of the sentence for emphasis and *אֶת*
is what stops the sentence from becoming ambiguous.

::: exercise Et or nothing
"the book", as an object || *אֶת הַסֵּפֶר* || definite, so *אֶת*
"a book", as an object || *סֵפֶר* || indefinite, so nothing
"David", as an object || *אֶת דָּוִד* || a name is definite without an article
"my notebook", as an object || *אֶת הַמַּחְבֶּרֶת שֶׁלִּי* || a possessive makes it definite
Say "I have the notebook" || *יֵשׁ לִי אֶת הַמַּחְבֶּרֶת* || compare *יֵשׁ לִי מַחְבֶּרֶת*, no *אֶת*
"her", as an object || *אוֹתָהּ* || pronoun objects are always definite
"you", to a group of men, as an object || *אֶתְכֶם* || one of the two forms that keeps the short stem
What does *אֶת* mean? || nothing || it marks a definite direct object and carries no meaning of its own
:::

# Vav and yod as vowels

Real Hebrew has no nikud in it. Signs, newspapers, menus, messages: all of
them are bare consonants, and the reader supplies the vowels. That would be
impossible if there were no clues at all, so written Hebrew leaves some in,
using two letters as stand-ins for vowels.

Those two letters are ו and י. Grammar calls them *אִמּוֹת קְרִיאָה*
/imot kri'a/, "mothers of reading", and using them generously is called
*כְּתִיב מָלֵא* /ktiv male/, full spelling. Writing without them, which is what
pointed text does, is *כְּתִיב חָסֵר* /ktiv khaser/, defective spelling.

## What each one stands for

| Letter | Stands for | Example with points | Written plainly |
|---|---|---|---|
| ו | o | *שָׁלוֹם* /shalom/ | שלום |
| ו | u | *שׁוּק* /shuk/ | שוק |
| י | i | *עִיר* /ir/ | עיר |
| י | e (from *צֵירֵי*) | *בֵּית* /bet/ | בית |

::: rule
A ו in an unpointed word is o or u far more often than it is the consonant v.
A י is i or e far more often than it is the consonant y. Try the vowel first.
:::

## The same word, two ways

This is the part that trips people up, so it is worth seeing directly. The
word for table can be written either way:

| Spelling | Style |
|---|---|
| *שֻׁלְחָן* | pointed, defective: the u is the *קוּבּוּץ* under the ש |
| *שׁוּלְחָן* | pointed, full: the u is the ו, and the sign moves into it |
| שולחן | unpointed, full: what you actually see in Israel |

All three are the same word. The third is what is on the furniture shop's
sign, and it is only readable because the ו is there to say "u".

::: warning
This course writes Hebrew in **full spelling with the points on top**:
*שׁוּלְחָן*, not *שֻׁלְחָן*. That is deliberate. Switch the nikud off with the
button in the top bar and what is left is שולחן, exactly what you will meet
outside. With defective spelling you would be left with שלחן, which nobody
writes.
:::

## The rules, roughly

The Academy of the Hebrew Language publishes a full set of spelling rules, and
they are in Part III for anyone who wants them. Four of them cover most cases,
and they are worth having now because they let you guess a spelling you have
not seen.

**1. o and u are written with ו.**

::: examples
טוֹב || good, written טוב
גָּדוֹל || big, written גדול
קוּם || get up, written קום
:::

**2. i is written with י**, unless the next letter is doubled.

::: examples
עִיר || a city, written עיר
מִיץ || juice, written מיץ
אִישׁ || a man, written איש
:::

**3. A *צֵירֵי* that already has a י keeps it. One that does not, usually does
not gain one.**

::: examples
בֵּית סֵפֶר || a school, written בית ספר: the first word has its yod, the second does not
:::

**4. Doubled letters are written twice: וו for a consonant v, יי for a
consonant y between vowels.**

::: examples
מִצְוָוה || a commandment, written מצווה
אֲוִויר || air, written אוויר
בִּנְיָינִים || verb patterns, written בניינים
:::

::: note
This is the reason a word like *בִּנְיָינִים* looks so crowded when pointed. The
double yod is there for the unpointed reader; the points on top are there for
you. Neither is redundant, they serve different readers, and full spelling
carries both.
:::

## What is still missing

Even with full spelling, a and e are mostly not written. That is why unpointed
Hebrew still needs a reader who knows the language:

| Unpointed | Could be | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ספר | *סֵפֶר* | a book |
| ספר | *סַפָּר* | a barber |
| ספר | *סִפֵּר* | he told |
| ספר | *סְפַר* | count! |

Four words, one spelling. In practice the confusion nearly never happens,
because the sentence around the word decides it. A book sits on a table, a
barber cuts hair, and a verb is where a verb belongs. Reading unpointed Hebrew
is reading in context, which is the whole of chapter 7.

::: tip
When you meet an unfamiliar unpointed word, read the consonants aloud with no
vowels at all and listen for what it nearly is. *ktvt* is *כְּתוֹבֶת* /ktovet/,
an address. The consonant skeleton carries more information than an English
speaker expects, because Hebrew roots are consonantal.
:::

::: exercise Full spelling
Write *טוֹב* without nikud || טוב || the ו stays: it is carrying the o
Write *שׁוּק* without nikud || שוק || the ו is the u
Write *עִיר* without nikud || עיר || the י is the i
Why is *מִצְוָוה* written with two vavs? || the first is a consonant v; one vav alone would be read as a vowel || the doubling is what marks it as a consonant
What does *כְּתִיב מָלֵא* mean, and is this course written in it? || full spelling, and yes || full spelling with the points on top, so switching them off leaves ordinary Hebrew
Read the unpointed word בית || bayit, a house, or beit in a compound || the י is the vowel, not a consonant
Give three words the unpointed spelling ספר could be || *סֵפֶר*, *סַפָּר*, *סִפֵּר* || a book, a barber, he told; context decides
:::

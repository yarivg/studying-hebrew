# Negation

Negation is one of the places where Hebrew is simpler than English and then,
at the last moment, stranger. The simple part: one word, *לֹא* /lo/, put
directly in front of whatever you are denying. The strange part comes at the
end of this chapter.

## לֹא before the verb

::: rule
*לֹא* goes immediately before the verb. Nothing goes between them, and nothing
else changes. There is no Hebrew "do not".
:::

English needs a helper verb it does not otherwise use: "I write" becomes "I do
not write". Hebrew has no equivalent of that "do", and looking for one is the
commonest beginner instinct to unlearn.

::: examples
אֲנִי כּוֹתֵב || I write
אֲנִי לֹא כּוֹתֵב || I do not write
הִיא לֹא מְדַבֶּרֶת עִבְרִית || she does not speak Hebrew
אֲנַחְנוּ לֹא רוֹצִים לִלְמוֹד || we do not want to study
הֵם לֹא גָּרִים כָּאן || they do not live here
:::

## לֹא before an adjective or a noun

Sentences with no verb in them, the ones from chapter 11 and chapter 12, are
negated in exactly the same way: *לֹא* goes in front of the predicate, which
is the adjective or the noun doing the describing.

::: examples
הוּא גָּדוֹל || he is big
הוּא לֹא גָּדוֹל || he is not big
הַקָּפֶה לֹא חַם || the coffee is not hot
הַדִּירָה לֹא גְּדוֹלָה || the flat is not big
הוּא לֹא מוֹרֶה, הוּא סְטוּדֶנְט || he is not a teacher, he is a student
זֶה לֹא נָכוֹן || that is not true
:::

Note where *לֹא* does not go: it never attaches to the subject. *לֹא הוּא
גָּדוֹל* is not "he is not big"; it is closer to "it is not *he* who is big",
which is a different sentence.

::: sound
*לֹא* is written לא, with a silent alef, and rhymes with "raw". It is never
shortened and never leans on the next word. It is a full, stressed syllable of
its own, which is part of why it is so easy to hear in speech.
:::

## אֵין: the negative of יֵשׁ

*לֹא* does not negate *יֵשׁ*. There is a dedicated word, *אֵין* /ein/, from
chapter 16, and it replaces *יֵשׁ* rather than sitting in front of it.

| Positive | Negative | |
|---|---|---|
| *יֵשׁ חָלָב* | *אֵין חָלָב* | there is no milk |
| *יֵשׁ לִי זְמַן* | *אֵין לִי זְמַן* | I have no time |
| *יֵשׁ כָּאן בַּיִת* | *אֵין כָּאן בַּיִת* | there is no house here |

::: warning
*לֹא יֵשׁ* does not exist. Neither does *אֵין לֹא*. One word does the whole
job, and hearing *לֹא יֵשׁ* marks a speaker as a beginner instantly.
:::

::: note
In formal and written Hebrew *אֵין* also negates a present-tense verb, with an
ending for the person: *אֵינִי יוֹדֵעַ*, "I do not know", *אֵינוֹ מְדַבֵּר*, "he
does not speak". Nobody talks like this. Recognise it on a page; say *לֹא*.
:::

## אַל: the negative command

To tell someone not to do something, Hebrew uses neither *לֹא* nor *אֵין*, but
*אַל* /al/, followed by a future-tense verb.

The future tense is Part II, so take these as whole phrases for now:

::: examples
אַל תִּדְאַג || don't worry (to a man)
אַל תִּדְאֲגִי || don't worry (to a woman)
אַל תִּשְׁכַּח || don't forget (to a man)
:::

*אַל* is only ever a command. Every statement about what does or does not
happen takes *לֹא*, whatever the tense.

## Nobody and nothing

*אַף אֶחָד* /af ekhad/ is "nobody" and *שׁוּם דָּבָר* /shum davar/ is "nothing".
Neither of them negates on its own. Each one needs *לֹא* in the sentence with
it.

::: examples
אַף אֶחָד לֹא בָּא || nobody comes, nobody is coming
אַף אֶחָד לֹא מְדַבֵּר אַנְגְּלִית || nobody speaks English
שׁוּם דָּבָר לֹא עוֹבֵד || nothing works
אַף פַּעַם לֹא || never
אֲנִי אַף פַּעַם לֹא שׁוֹתֶה קָפֶה || I never drink coffee
:::

::: rule
Hebrew requires the double negative that English forbids. *אַף אֶחָד לֹא בָּא*
is word for word "nobody does not come", and it means "nobody comes". Leaving
the *לֹא* out is not more correct; it is ungrammatical.
:::

This is the one thing in the chapter that genuinely has to be overridden
rather than learned, because English schooling has trained the instinct out of
you. Spanish, French and Russian all behave the way Hebrew does here, so if you
have any of those, lean on it.

::: warning
*אַף אֶחָד* is masculine in form but covers everybody, men and women together,
and it is what you say by default. *אַף אַחַת* exists and is used when the
group being talked about is explicitly female.
:::

## Not yet, and not any more

Two very frequent pairs, both built out of *לֹא* and a small word in front of
it.

| Hebrew | English | |
|---|---|---|
| *עוֹד לֹא* | not yet | something expected has not happened |
| *עֲדַיִין לֹא* | not yet | the same thing, slightly more formal |
| *כְּבָר לֹא* | not any more | it used to be true and has stopped |

::: examples
הוּא עוֹד לֹא כָּאן || he is not here yet
אֲנִי עוֹד לֹא מְבִינָה || I do not understand yet
אֲנִי כְּבָר לֹא גָּר בְּתֵל אָבִיב || I do not live in Tel Aviv any more
הִיא כְּבָר לֹא קְטַנָּה || she is not little any more
:::

The logic is worth seeing: *עוֹד* is "still" and *כְּבָר* is "already", so
*עוֹד לֹא* is "still not" and *כְּבָר לֹא* is "already not". Hebrew builds both
out of parts you can see.

::: exercise Say no
Negate *אֲנִי קוֹרֵא סֵפֶר* || *אֲנִי לֹא קוֹרֵא סֵפֶר* || *לֹא* immediately before the verb
Negate *הַקָּפֶה חַם* || *הַקָּפֶה לֹא חַם* || no verb needed, *לֹא* goes before the adjective
Negate *יֵשׁ לִי זְמַן* || *אֵין לִי זְמַן* || *אֵין* replaces *יֵשׁ*; *לֹא יֵשׁ* does not exist
Say "nobody is coming" || *אַף אֶחָד לֹא בָּא* || the *לֹא* is compulsory
Say "nothing works" || *שׁוּם דָּבָר לֹא עוֹבֵד* || the same double negative
Say "I never drink coffee" (a woman) || *אֲנִי אַף פַּעַם לֹא שׁוֹתָה קָפֶה* || *אַף פַּעַם* also needs *לֹא*
What is wrong with *אַף אֶחָד בָּא*? || the *לֹא* is missing || Hebrew doubles the negative where English does not
Which word tells someone not to do something? || *אַל* || and it takes the future tense, which is Part II
:::

# Past tense: pa'al

The present tense gave you four forms and asked one question: masculine or
feminine, singular or plural. The past asks a different question, and asks it
nine times. Hebrew's past marks **who did it**, not just what gender they
were, and the marking goes on the end of the verb.

## Nine endings, one stem

Here is *לִכְתּוֹב* /likhtov/, to write, in the past. The stem is *כָּתַב* and
everything after it is an ending.

| Pronoun | Past | |
|---|---|---|
| אֲנִי | כָּתַבְתִּי | I wrote |
| אַתָּה | כָּתַבְתָּ | you wrote (m) |
| אַתְּ | כָּתַבְתְּ | you wrote (f) |
| הוּא | כָּתַב | he wrote |
| הִיא | כָּתְבָה | she wrote |
| אֲנַחְנוּ | כָּתַבְנוּ | we wrote |
| אַתֶּם | כְּתַבְתֶּם | you wrote (m pl) |
| אַתֶּן | כְּתַבְתֶּן | you wrote (f pl) |
| הֵם / הֵן | כָּתְבוּ | they wrote |

Read the endings on their own: *ְתִּי*, *ְתָּ*, *ְתְּ*, nothing at all, *ָה*,
*ְנוּ*, *ְתֶּם*, *ְתֶּן*, *וּ*. That short list is the entire Hebrew past
tense. It does not change from verb to verb and, as the next chapter shows, it
does not change from binyan to binyan either. Learn it once.

::: rule
The ending carries the person. In the first and second person the pronoun is
therefore optional: *כָּתַבְתִּי* already says "I".
:::

::: examples
כָּתַבְתִּי מִכְתָּב || I wrote a letter
אֲנִי כָּתַבְתִּי אֶת הַמִּכְתָּב || I am the one who wrote the letter
לָמָּה לֹא כָּתַבְתְּ? || why didn't you write? (to a woman)
הוּא כָּתַב וְהִיא קָרְאָה || he wrote and she read
:::

Putting the pronoun back in is not a mistake, it is emphasis: *אֲנִי כָּתַבְתִּי*
answers "who wrote this?". The third person is different. Nothing inside
*כָּתַב* says whether he, the teacher or the dog did the writing, so the third
person keeps its subject.

## Why the dictionary says כָּתַב

*כָּתַב* is the third person masculine singular, and it is also the barest
shape the root can take: three letters with an *a* after each of the first
two, and no ending at all. That is why a dictionary, and almost every grammar
book written in Hebrew, cites a verb as *כָּתַב* rather than as an infinitive.

The *בִּנְיָין* /binyan/ names are built the same way. *פָּעַל* /pa'al/ is the
root פ-ע-ל poured into that exact shape, and on its own it means "he did".
The binyan is named after its own third person masculine singular past.

This course cites the infinitive, *לִכְתּוֹב*, because that is the form you
actually say after *רוֹצֶה* or *צָרִיךְ*. Be ready to meet the other
convention everywhere else.

## Two more sound verbs

Nothing new happens. Only the three letters change.

| Pronoun | *לִסְגּוֹר* /lisgor/ to close | *לִלְמוֹד* /lilmod/ to learn |
|---|---|---|
| אֲנִי | סָגַרְתִּי | לָמַדְתִּי |
| אַתָּה | סָגַרְתָּ | לָמַדְתָּ |
| אַתְּ | סָגַרְתְּ | לָמַדְתְּ |
| הוּא | סָגַר | לָמַד |
| הִיא | סָגְרָה | לָמְדָה |
| אֲנַחְנוּ | סָגַרְנוּ | לָמַדְנוּ |
| אַתֶּם | סְגַרְתֶּם | לְמַדְתֶּם |
| אַתֶּן | סְגַרְתֶּן | לְמַדְתֶּן |
| הֵם / הֵן | סָגְרוּ | לָמְדוּ |

Two vowel details are worth noticing, because they repeat across every verb in
this chapter. The *הִיא* and the plural forms lose the second *a*: *כָּתְבָה*,
not *כָּתַבָה*. And the *אַתֶּם* and *אַתֶּן* forms lose the first one.

## What people actually say

*כְּתַבְתֶּם* and *כְּתַבְתֶּן* are the only two forms that open with a shva,
and the stress lands on the ending. Almost nobody talks that way. In speech
you will hear *כָּתַבְתֶּם* /katavtem/, with the stem vowel restored and the
stress pulled back onto the stem. The Academy prefers the first form; a news
broadcast, a lecture and an argument on the bus all use the second.

::: warning
*אַתֶּן* and its *ְתֶּן* ending are going the way of the rest of the feminine
plural. Most Israelis say *כְּתַבְתֶּם* to a room full of women. This course
teaches all nine forms; expect to hear eight.
:::

::: sound
*כָּתַבְתָּ* and *כָּתַבְתְּ* are both כתבת once the points come off. Aloud
they are not close: /katavta/ ends on a vowel, /katavt/ stops dead on the t.
Blur it and you have addressed a man as a woman.
:::

## One Hebrew past, four English ones

::: note
*כָּתַבְתִּי* covers "I wrote", "I was writing", "I have written" and "I had
written". Hebrew has one past tense and lets context sort out the rest. Do not
go looking for a way to say the difference; there isn't one.
:::

Negation needs nothing new. *לֹא* goes in front of the verb and nothing else
moves: *לֹא לָמַדְתִּי*, I didn't learn. Hebrew has no auxiliary "did".

::: exercise Put it in the past
I wrote (say it without the pronoun) || *כָּתַבְתִּי* || the ending already means "I"
she closed || *הִיא סָגְרָה* || third person keeps its pronoun
we learned || *לָמַדְנוּ* || *ְנוּ* for *אֲנַחְנוּ*
they wrote || *הֵם כָּתְבוּ* || one plural form for both genders
you (f) wrote || *כָּתַבְתְּ* || no vowel after the final ת
Why is *הוּא כָּתַב* not just *כָּתַב*? || the third person has no personal ending, so it needs a subject || first and second person do not
Why do dictionaries list *כָּתַב*? || it is the bare root in the a-a shape, with no ending on it || the binyan *פָּעַל* is named the same way
What does an Israeli say instead of *כְּתַבְתֶּם*? || *כָּתַבְתֶּם*, stressed on the stem || the Academy still prefers *כְּתַבְתֶּם*
:::

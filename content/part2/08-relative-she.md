# The relative ש

English needs three words to attach a clause to a noun: who for people, which
for things, that for either. Hebrew needs one letter. *שֶׁ* /she/ glues onto
the front of the next word, with no space, and does all three jobs at once.

::: examples
הָאִישׁ שֶׁגָּר לְיָדִי || the man who lives next to me
הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁקָּנִיתִי || the book that I bought
הַמִּכְתָּב שֶׁהִגִּיעַ אֶתְמוֹל || the letter which arrived yesterday
הַיְלָדִים שֶׁמְּדַבְּרִים עִבְרִית || the children who speak Hebrew
:::

Note the dagesh it puts into the following letter where one can go:
*שֶׁקָּנִיתִי*, *שֶׁמְּדַבְּרִים*. That is *שֶׁ* behaving like the article *הַ*,
and you do not have to do anything about it in speech.

## It is never optional

::: rule
English can drop "that": the book I bought. Hebrew cannot drop *שֶׁ*, ever.
:::

*הָאִישׁ רָאִיתִי* is not a clumsy sentence, it is not a sentence. It reads as
two fragments jammed together. Say *הָאִישׁ שֶׁרָאִיתִי*. This is the single
most common English-speaker error in the whole area, because the English
original has no word there to translate.

::: note
The formal free-standing equivalent is *אֲשֶׁר* /asher/, a separate word:
*הָאִישׁ אֲשֶׁר רָאִיתִי*. It means exactly the same thing and belongs to
contracts, literature and the Bible. Read it, do not say it.
:::

## The pronoun Hebrew leaves behind

This is the part that runs against the grain. When the noun would be the
object of the relative clause, or would sit after a preposition inside it,
Hebrew keeps a pronoun in that slot.

::: examples
הַבַּיִת שֶׁגַּרְתִּי בּוֹ || the house I lived in
הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁדִּיבַּרְנוּ עָלָיו || the book we talked about
הָאִישׁ שֶׁדִּיבַּרְתִּי אִיתּוֹ || the man I spoke with
הַמִּסְעָדָה שֶׁאָכַלְנוּ בָּהּ || the restaurant we ate in
הַיַּלְדָּה שֶׁנָּתַתִּי לָהּ אֶת הַסֵּפֶר || the girl I gave the book to
:::

Look at what English did to the same sentences. "The house I lived in" has an
*in* with nothing after it, a preposition left dangling because its noun moved
to the front. Hebrew refuses to leave a preposition hanging. The preposition
stays where it was and takes the ending that points back at the noun:
*בּוֹ*, *עָלָיו*, *אִיתּוֹ*, *בָּהּ*, *לָהּ*.

::: rule
After a preposition the resumptive pronoun is obligatory. *הַבַּיִת שֶׁגַּרְתִּי*
is missing its *בּוֹ* and means nothing.
:::

With a plain direct object it is softer: both are said, and the version with
the pronoun is the more usual when the noun is definite.

::: examples
הָאִישׁ שֶׁרָאִיתִי || the man I saw
הָאִישׁ שֶׁרָאִיתִי אוֹתוֹ || the man I saw
:::

::: warning
An English speaker's instinct is to delete this pronoun, because English
deletes it: "the man I saw him" is broken English. In Hebrew it is correct,
and after a preposition it is the only option. Train the instinct backwards.
:::

## מַה שֶּׁ and מִי שֶׁ

*שֶׁ* also attaches to a question word to make "what" or "whoever" in the sense
of a headless clause.

::: examples
מַה שֶּׁאָמַרְתִּי || what I said
זֶה מַה שֶּׁיֵּשׁ || that's what there is
כָּל מַה שֶּׁרָצִיתִי || everything I wanted
מִי שֶׁרוֹצֶה, שֶׁיָּבוֹא || whoever wants to, let them come
:::

::: warning
*מַה* on its own asks; *מַה שֶּׁ* points at a thing. *מַה קָּרָה?* is "what
happened?", *סַפֵּר לִי מַה שֶּׁקָּרָה* is "tell me what happened". Where the
English "what" could be replaced by "the thing that", Hebrew needs the *שֶׁ*:
*מַה שֶּׁאָמַרְתִּי נָכוֹן*, what I said is true.
:::

## The other job: that

The same letter introduces a whole clause after a verb of saying, thinking or
feeling. Here it is not attached to a noun at all.

::: examples
הוּא אָמַר שֶׁהוּא עָסוּק || he said that he is busy
אֲנִי חוֹשֵׁב שֶׁזֶּה נָכוֹן || I think that this is right
יָדַעְתִּי שֶׁתָּבוֹא || I knew you would come
אֲנִי מְקַוֶּוה שֶׁהַכֹּל בְּסֵדֶר || I hope everything is fine
הִיא בְּטוּחָה שֶׁהוּא צוֹדֵק || she is sure he is right
:::

English drops this "that" freely and Hebrew still cannot drop *שֶׁ*. Same rule,
same reason, same mistake.

::: tip
Newspapers write *כִּי* /ki/ where speech says *שֶׁ* after a verb of saying:
*הַשַּׂר אָמַר כִּי*, the minister said that. In every other context *כִּי*
means "because", so read the verb in front of it before deciding.
:::

::: exercise Attach the clause
Say "the book that I bought" || *הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁקָּנִיתִי* || *שֶׁ* is written onto the next word
Say "the man I saw" || *הָאִישׁ שֶׁרָאִיתִי* || English drops "that", Hebrew never does
Say "the house I lived in" || *הַבַּיִת שֶׁגַּרְתִּי בּוֹ* || the preposition stays inside and takes an ending
Say "the restaurant we ate in" || *הַמִּסְעָדָה שֶׁאָכַלְנוּ בָּהּ* || feminine noun, so *בָּהּ*
What is wrong with *הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁדִּיבַּרְנוּ*? || the resumptive pronoun is missing || it needs *עָלָיו*
Say "what I said" || *מַה שֶּׁאָמַרְתִּי* || *מַה* alone would be a question
Say "he said that he is busy" || *הוּא אָמַר שֶׁהוּא עָסוּק* || the other job of *שֶׁ*
Give the formal written equivalent of *שֶׁ* || *אֲשֶׁר* || a separate word, and not for speech
:::

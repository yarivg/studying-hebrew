# Possession: shel and the endings

Hebrew has two ways to say "my book". One is what everyone says out loud. The
other is what a newspaper prints, a poem uses, and a handful of very common
words insist on even in the kitchen. Learn the first properly. Learn to read
the second.

## shel with an ending

*שֶׁל* /shel/ is the possession word, and on its own it sits between two nouns:
*הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁל דָּנִי*, Danny's book, literally "the book of Danny". Put a
pronoun ending on it and you have the whole system.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | *שֶׁלִּי* my | *שֶׁלָּנוּ* our |
| 2nd m. | *שֶׁלְּךָ* your | *שֶׁלָּכֶם* your |
| 2nd f. | *שֶׁלָּךְ* your | *שֶׁלָּכֶן* your |
| 3rd m. | *שֶׁלּוֹ* his | *שֶׁלָּהֶם* their |
| 3rd f. | *שֶׁלָּהּ* her | *שֶׁלָּהֶן* their |

These are the same endings that went on *לְ* in chapter 17 of Part I, so the
shape should already be familiar. The dagesh in the ל is not decoration: it is
the reason *שֶׁלִּי* /sheli/ is one word and not two.

::: examples
הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי || my book
הַמַּחְשֵׁב שֶׁלְּךָ || your computer (to a man)
הַדִּירָה שֶׁלָּךְ || your flat (to a woman)
הַחֲבֵרִים שֶׁלּוֹ || his friends
הָאוֹטוֹ שֶׁלָּנוּ || our car
הַכֶּלֶב שֶׁלָּהֶם || their dog
:::

## The article stays

::: rule
The noun keeps its *הַ*. It is *הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי*, never *סֵפֶר שֶׁלִּי*.
:::

English drops "the" in front of "my", because "my" already tells you which
book. Hebrew does not: the thing is definite, so it is marked definite, and
*שֶׁלִּי* is added on top. Leaving the *הַ* off is the single commonest mistake
English speakers make with this construction, and it does not sound like a
small slip. *סֵפֶר שֶׁלִּי* reads as "a book of mine", which is a different and
usually unwanted sentence.

::: warning
Watch what happens when an adjective joins. Definiteness has to agree, so all
three parts line up: *הַסֵּפֶר הֶחָדָשׁ שֶׁלִּי*, my new book. The *שֶׁלִּי* goes
last, after the adjective, not between the noun and it.
:::

## The ending on the noun itself

Older Hebrew did not use *שֶׁל* at all. It glued the same endings straight onto
the noun, and that construction is alive in writing.

| Person | *סֵפֶר* book | *בַּיִת* house | *שֵׁם* name |
|---|---|---|---|
| my | *סִפְרִי* | *בֵּיתִי* | *שְׁמִי* |
| your m. | *סִפְרְךָ* | *בֵּיתְךָ* | *שִׁמְךָ* |
| your f. | *סִפְרֵךְ* | *בֵּיתֵךְ* | *שְׁמֵךְ* |
| his | *סִפְרוֹ* | *בֵּיתוֹ* | *שְׁמוֹ* |
| her | *סִפְרָהּ* | *בֵּיתָהּ* | *שְׁמָהּ* |
| our | *סִפְרֵנוּ* | *בֵּיתֵנוּ* | *שְׁמֵנוּ* |
| your pl. | *סִפְרְכֶם* | *בֵּיתְכֶם* | *שִׁמְכֶם* |
| their m. | *סִפְרָם* | *בֵּיתָם* | *שְׁמָם* |

Notice that the noun itself changes shape before the ending arrives: *סֵפֶר*
gives *סִפְרִי*, the same reshuffle it performs in the plural *סְפָרִים*. The
ending is added to a stem, not to the dictionary word.

On a plural noun the endings are different again, and they all contain a yod:

::: examples
סְפָרַיי || my books
סְפָרֶיךָ || your books (to a man)
סְפָרַיִיךְ || your books (to a woman)
סְפָרָיו || his books
סְפָרֶיהָ || her books
סְפָרֵינוּ || our books
סִפְרֵיהֶם || their books
:::

::: note
*סְפָרַיי* ends in a doubled yod, and so do *עָלַיי* and *לְפָנַיי* in the next
chapter. That is full spelling: with one yod the word would be read as
*סִפְרִי*, my book, singular.
:::

::: warning
*סְפָרָיו* and *סְפָרֶיהָ* are ספריו and ספריה once the points come off, and a
reader has to tell "his books" from "her books" out of context alone. This is
a genuine difficulty, not a beginner's problem that goes away. You will meet
the same pair again in the next chapter with *עָלָיו* and *עָלֶיהָ*.
:::

## Where the old endings survive in speech

The suffixed form is formal, except in three pockets where it is what an
Israeli actually says.

**Family.** These take the ending in ordinary conversation.

::: examples
אִמִּי || my mother
אָבִי || my father
אָחִי || my brother
אֲחוֹתִי || my sister
בְּנִי || my son
בִּתִּי || my daughter
:::

Even here *אִימָּא שֶׁלִּי* /ima sheli/ is more common in casual speech, and
*אִמִּי* sounds a touch weightier, the way "my mother" does next to "my mum".
But nobody blinks at it.

**Body parts inside idioms.** The body part is not really the point of the
phrase; the phrase is frozen whole.

::: examples
בְּעֵינַיי || in my eyes, in my view
בְּלִיבִּי || in my heart
מֵאֲחוֹרֵי גַּבִּי || behind my back
:::

**Set phrases.** Learn them as vocabulary, not as grammar.

::: examples
לְדַעְתִּי || in my opinion
לְמַזָּלִי || luckily for me
בִּרְשׁוּתְךָ || with your permission
בְּבַקָּשָׁתִי || at my request (formal writing only)
:::

::: note
*תּוֹדָה רַבָּה* is not one of these. The *רַבָּה* is an ordinary adjective
agreeing with the feminine *תּוֹדָה*, and there is no ending on anything.
:::

::: rule
Produce *שֶׁל*. Recognise the suffix. That division holds until you are reading
literature, and then the tables above are here to come back to.
:::

Say "my book" || *הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי* || the *הַ* stays on the noun

Say "her flat" || *הַדִּירָה שֶׁלָּהּ* || the ה of *שֶׁלָּהּ* carries a dot inside it
Say "my new book" || *הַסֵּפֶר הֶחָדָשׁ שֶׁלִּי* || noun, adjective, then *שֶׁלִּי*

Why is *אִמִּי* not strange in speech? || family words keep the old endings || the same for *אָבִי*, *אָחִי*, *בְּנִי*, *בִּתִּי*
:::

# Direction and place

Hebrew marks the difference between being somewhere and going somewhere, and
it marks it twice: once with a living system of prepositions, and once with a
suffix left over from an older stage of the language.

## The directional he

Add an unstressed *ָה* to certain nouns and it means "to" or "towards". The
stress stays where it was, which is the give-away: *בַּיִת* /bayit/ becomes
*הַבַּיְתָה* /habayta/, not *habaytá*.

::: examples
הַבַּיְתָה || home, to the house
הָעִירָה || into town
צָפוֹנָה || northwards
דָּרוֹמָה || southwards
מִזְרָחָה || eastwards
מַעֲרָבָה || westwards
:::

The same ending gives the four directions you need in a car:

::: examples
יָמִינָה || to the right
שְׂמֹאלָה || to the left
קָדִימָה || forwards
אָחוֹרָה || backwards
:::

::: rule
This is a closed set. You cannot invent a new one. *לַסּוּפֶּרְמַרְקֶט* is how
you say "to the supermarket"; *סוּפֶּרְמַרְקֶטָה* is not a word and will get you
a blank look.
:::

The list above is very nearly the whole of it in everyday speech, along with
*הַחוּצָה* /hachutza/, outwards, and *אַרְצָה*, to the Land, which is what
immigration to Israel is called: *עָלִיתִי אַרְצָה*, I came up to the Land.

::: tip
*יָמִינָה* and *שְׂמֹאלָה* are the words a taxi driver is waiting for, and
*קָדִימָה* doubles as "let's go". Learn these four before the compass points.
:::

## be, le, el

Now the live system. Three prepositions, and the line between them is about
what the verb is doing.

::: rule
*בְּ* for being somewhere. *לְ* for going somewhere. *אֶל* for going towards
someone, or towards a destination in a more formal register.
:::

::: examples
אֲנִי בַּבַּיִת || I am at home
אֲנִי הוֹלֵךְ לַבַּיִת || I am going home
אֲנִי הוֹלֵךְ הַבַּיְתָה || I am going home
פָּנִיתִי אֵלָיו || I turned to him
הוּא נָסַע לְתֵל אָבִיב || he travelled to Tel Aviv
הִיא גָּרָה בְּתֵל אָבִיב || she lives in Tel Aviv
:::

*לְ* and *אֶל* overlap almost completely for places, and *לְ* is what you say.
*אֶל* is the one you write, and the one that sounds right in front of a
person: *בָּאתִי אֵלֶיךָ*, I came to you, where *לְךָ* would mean something
else entirely. The endings on *אֶל* came in the last chapter.

::: warning
*הַבַּיְתָה* against *בַּבַּיִת* is the confusion that costs an English speaker
most often, because English says "home" for both. *אֲנִי בַּבַּיִת* is I am at
home. *אֲנִי הוֹלֵךְ הַבַּיְתָה* is I am going home. Say *אֲנִי הוֹלֵךְ
בַּבַּיִת* and you have said you are walking around inside the house, which is
a real sentence and not the one you meant.
:::

## Where things are

The rest of place is prepositions, and several of them are compounds that drag
a *לְ* or a *מִ* along behind them. That second word is not optional.

| Hebrew | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *עַל* | on | *הַסֵּפֶר עַל הַשּׁוּלְחָן* |
| *מִתַּחַת לְ* | under | *הֶחָתוּל מִתַּחַת לַשּׁוּלְחָן* |
| *מֵעַל* | above | *הַתְּמוּנָה מֵעַל הַסַּפָּה* |
| *לְיַד* | next to | *אֲנִי גָּר לְיַד הַיָּם* |
| *בֵּין* | between | *הַבַּנְק בֵּין הַדּוֹאַר לְבֵין בֵּית הַקָּפֶה* |
| *מוּל* | opposite, facing | *הַגַּן מוּל הַבַּיִת שֶׁלָּנוּ* |
| *בְּתוֹךְ* | inside | *הַמַּפְתֵּחַ בְּתוֹךְ הַתִּיק* |
| *מִחוּץ לְ* | outside | *הֵם גָּרִים מִחוּץ לָעִיר* |
| *אֵצֶל* | at someone's place | *הָיִיתִי אֵצֶל הָרוֹפֵא* |
| *קָרוֹב לְ* | near to | *הַמָּלוֹן קָרוֹב לַתַּחֲנָה* |
| *רָחוֹק מִ* | far from | *זֶה רָחוֹק מֵהַמֶּרְכָּז* |

::: note
*בֵּין* takes two things, and Hebrew links them with *לְבֵין* or with a plain
*וּ*: *בֵּין הַבַּיִת לְבֵין הַגַּן*, or *בֵּין הַבַּיִת וְהַגַּן*. Both are
heard. The *לְבֵין* version is the careful one.
:::

*עַל יַד* and *לְיַד* are the same word wearing two spellings, both meaning
"beside". Nothing turns on the choice.

::: warning
*אֵצֶל* has no clean English equivalent, which is why learners avoid it and
then sound stilted. It means "at the place of a person": *אֵצֶל הָרוֹפֵא*, at
the doctor's, *אֶצְלִי*, at my place, *אֵצֶל הַהוֹרִים*, at my parents'. It
takes the singular endings from Part I, not the plural set.
:::

::: exercise In or to
Say "I am at home" || *אֲנִי בַּבַּיִת* || *בְּ* for being somewhere
Say "I am going home" || *אֲנִי הוֹלֵךְ הַבַּיְתָה* || or *לַבַּיִת*, which is equally good
What does *יָמִינָה* mean? || to the right || the directional ending, unstressed
Why is *סוּפֶּרְמַרְקֶטָה* wrong? || the directional ending is a closed set || use *לַסּוּפֶּרְמַרְקֶט*
Say "the cat is under the table" || *הֶחָתוּל מִתַּחַת לַשּׁוּלְחָן* || *מִתַּחַת* needs its *לְ*
Say "I was at the doctor's" || *הָיִיתִי אֵצֶל הָרוֹפֵא* || *אֵצֶל* is "at the place of"
Say "she lives far from the centre" || *הִיא גָּרָה רָחוֹק מֵהַמֶּרְכָּז* || *רָחוֹק* pairs with *מִ*
Which is more formal, *לְ* or *אֶל*? || *אֶל* || but *אֶל* is normal in front of a person
:::

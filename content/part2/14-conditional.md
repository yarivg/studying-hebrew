# If

English has one word, "if", and buries the difference between a real condition
and an impossible one inside the tenses. Hebrew splits the work differently:
one word for conditions that might come true, another for the ones that will
not, and a tense rule that catches every English speaker once.

## Real conditions: im plus the future

::: rule
After *אִם* /im/, a future condition takes the **future tense**, in both halves
of the sentence.
:::

::: examples
אִם יֵרֵד גֶּשֶׁם, נִישָּׁאֵר בַּבַּיִת || if it rains, we will stay home
אִם תָּבוֹא מָחָר, נֵלֵךְ יַחַד || if you come tomorrow, we will go together
אִם יִהְיֶה לִי זְמַן, אֲנִי אֶתְקַשֵּׁר || if I have time, I will call
אִם לֹא תֹּאכַל, תִּהְיֶה רָעֵב || if you do not eat, you will be hungry
:::

Look at the English again: "if it rains", "if you come", "if I have". English
puts the present tense in the *if* half and the future in the other half.
Hebrew puts the future in both. Saying *אִם יוֹרֵד גֶּשֶׁם* is not a disaster,
but it shifts the meaning towards "if it is raining, right now", and an
Israeli hears the shift.

::: warning
*אִם* and *עִם* /im/ sound identical and are one letter apart on the page: אם
against עם. *אִם* is "if", *עִם* is "with". Nothing in the pronunciation helps
you, only the sentence.
:::

A condition about the past uses the past in both halves, and behaves the way
English does:

::: examples
אִם אָמַרְתָּ שֶׁתָּבוֹא, אֲנִי מְחַכֶּה || if you said you would come, I am waiting
אִם שָׁכַחְתָּ, אֵין בְּעָיָה || if you forgot, no problem
:::

## Unreal conditions: im plus haya

For something that is not true, or was not true, Hebrew uses *הָיָה* /haya/ in
the past, agreeing with the subject, followed by the present participle. Both
halves do it.

::: examples
אִם הָיִיתִי עָשִׁיר, הָיִיתִי קוֹנֶה בַּיִת || if I were rich, I would buy a house
אִם הָיָה לִי זְמַן, הָיִיתִי בָּא || if I had time, I would come
אִם הִיא הָיְתָה יוֹדַעַת, הִיא הָיְתָה אוֹמֶרֶת לָנוּ || if she knew, she would tell us
אִם הָיִינוּ יוֹצְאִים מוּקְדָּם, לֹא הָיִינוּ מְאַחֲרִים || if we had left early, we would not have been late
:::

::: rule
The same *הָיָה* plus participle does "if I were" and "if I had been". Hebrew
does not distinguish an unreal present from an unreal past; the time comes
from context or from an adverb.
:::

That last example is the proof. *אִם הָיִינוּ יוֹצְאִים* could in principle be
"if we were leaving", but *מוּקְדָּם* and the second half push it into the past,
and no Israeli would hesitate. If you need to be explicit, add *אֶתְמוֹל* or
*אָז*.

## ilu and lu

Formal Hebrew has a dedicated word for the unreal condition, and it takes the
same *הָיָה* construction.

::: examples
אִילּוּ יָדַעְתִּי, הָיִיתִי אוֹמֵר לְךָ || had I known, I would have told you
אִילּוּ הָיִיתִי בִּמְקוֹמְךָ || if I were in your place
לוּ הָיָה זֶה נָכוֹן || were that true
:::

*אִילּוּ* /ilu/ is written Hebrew and careful speech. *לוּ* /lu/ is higher
still, and you will meet it mostly in set phrases and in writing. Using *אִם*
where a writer would use *אִילּוּ* is never wrong, only plainer.

::: note
*לוּ* survives in one phrase everyone says: *הַלְוַאי*, if only, which is a
wish rather than a condition. *הַלְוַאי שֶׁתָּבוֹא*, I wish you would come.
:::

## im against ksheh

::: rule
*כְּשֶׁ* /ksheh/ is "when it happens". *אִם* is "if it happens". Choose by
whether the thing is certain.
:::

::: examples
כְּשֶׁאֶגְמוֹר, אֲנִי אֶתְקַשֵּׁר || when I finish, I will call
אִם אֶגְמוֹר, אֲנִי אֶתְקַשֵּׁר || if I finish, I will call
כְּשֶׁהוּא הִגִּיעַ, כּוּלָּם קָמוּ || when he arrived, everyone stood up
:::

English "when" about the future has to be *כְּשֶׁ*, and it takes the future
tense after it exactly as *אִם* does: *כְּשֶׁאָבוֹא*, when I come. English says
"when I come" with a present tense there too, so the same trap is waiting on
this word as on the last one.

## Unless, and two frozen phrases

*אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן* is "unless". It is four words long and it is the normal way to
say it.

::: examples
לֹא אָבוֹא, אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן תִּתְקַשֵּׁר || I will not come unless you call
נֵצֵא בְּשֵׁשׁ, אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן יֵרֵד גֶּשֶׁם || we will leave at six unless it rains
:::

And two short phrases that are *אִם* used as a connector rather than a
condition, both meaning roughly "in that case, so then":

::: examples
אִם כָּךְ, נִיפָּגֵשׁ מָחָר || in that case, we will meet tomorrow
אִם כֵּן, מָה הַבְּעָיָה || if so, what is the problem
:::

::: exercise Real and unreal
Say "if it rains, we will stay home" || *אִם יֵרֵד גֶּשֶׁם, נִישָּׁאֵר בַּבַּיִת* || future in both halves
Why is *אִם יוֹרֵד גֶּשֶׁם* odd here? || the present shifts it to "if it is raining now" || a future condition needs the future
Say "if I were rich, I would buy a house" || *אִם הָיִיתִי עָשִׁיר, הָיִיתִי קוֹנֶה בַּיִת* || *הָיָה* plus the participle, twice
Which word is formal, *אִם* or *אִילּוּ*? || *אִילּוּ* || same construction after it
"When I finish, I will call" || *כְּשֶׁאֶגְמוֹר, אֲנִי אֶתְקַשֵּׁר* || certain, so *כְּשֶׁ*, and still the future
Say "unless you call" || *אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן תִּתְקַשֵּׁר* || one unit, learn it whole
What is the difference between אם and עם? || *אִם* is "if", *עִם* is "with" || identical in speech
What does *הַלְוַאי* mean? || if only, I wish || a wish, not a condition
:::

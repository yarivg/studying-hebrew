# Adverbs and connectors

English builds adverbs on an assembly line: take an adjective, add -ly, done.
Hebrew has no such ending. It has three separate strategies instead, and the
first one is to not build an adverb at all.

## Three ways to say how

**1. Use the masculine singular adjective unchanged.** It never agrees with
anything, because it is describing the verb and not the subject.

::: examples
הוּא רָץ מַהֵר || he runs fast
הִיא רָצָה מַהֵר || she runs fast
הֵם עוֹבְדִים קָשֶׁה || they work hard
הוּא מְדַבֵּר יָפֶה || he speaks nicely
דִּיבַּרְתִּי חָזָק מִדַּי || I spoke too loudly
:::

Because the adjective is frozen in the masculine singular, the same sentence
works for a man, a woman or a crowd. There is nothing to get wrong, and an
English speaker's instinct to make it agree is the only thing to suppress.

**2. Use *בְּ* plus an abstract noun.** This is the productive strategy, the
nearest thing Hebrew has to -ly. Most of these nouns end in *וּת*, and if you
know the noun you can build the adverb on the spot.

::: examples
בְּשֶׁקֶט || quietly
בִּזְהִירוּת || carefully
בְּקַלּוּת || easily
בִּמְהִירוּת || quickly
בְּשִׂמְחָה || gladly
בְּרָצוֹן || willingly
בְּקוֹשִׁי || barely, with difficulty
:::

**3. Learn the fixed ones.** A short list that is not built from anything.

::: examples
מְאוֹד || very
לְאַט || slowly
טוֹב || well
רַע || badly
הֵיטֵב || well, in careful speech
:::

::: warning
*הוּא מְדַבֵּר טוֹב* is what everyone says and what a careful editor changes to
*הוּא מְדַבֵּר הֵיטֵב*. Use *טוֹב* in speech without worrying. Recognise
*הֵיטֵב* /hetev/ when you read it.
:::

## How often

| | |
|---|---|
| תָּמִיד | always |
| בְּדֶרֶךְ כְּלָל | usually |
| לְעִיתִּים קְרוֹבוֹת | often |
| לִפְעָמִים | sometimes |
| כִּמְעַט אַף פַּעַם לֹא | almost never |
| אַף פַּעַם לֹא | never |

::: rule
*אַף פַּעַם* needs *לֹא* with it. *אַף פַּעַם לֹא הָיִיתִי שָׁם*, I have never
been there, with both negatives. Hebrew stacks them where English cancels
them.
:::

## When

::: examples
כְּבָר אָכַלְתִּי || I have already eaten
הוּא עֲדַיִין כָּאן || he is still here
עוֹד לֹא סִיַּימְתִּי || I haven't finished yet
תִּתְקַשֵּׁר שׁוּב || call again
אֲנִי בָּא מִיָּד || I'm coming right away
תֵּיכֶף נִגְמַר || it'll be over in a moment
סוֹף סוֹף || finally, at last
:::

*כְּבָר* and *עֲדַיִין* between them carry most of what English does with the
perfect tense, which Hebrew does not have: *כְּבָר אָכַלְתִּי* is a past tense
doing the work of "I have eaten". *עוֹד* is the awkward one: alone it means
more or still, with *לֹא* in front of the verb it means not yet.

::: note
*תֵּיכֶף* and *מִיָּד* both mean immediately. *מִיָּד* is the honest one.
*תֵּיכֶף* in a café means "within some number of minutes", and in a queue it
means nothing at all.
:::

## Joining sentences

These are the words that turn a page of short statements into a paragraph. All
of them sit where the English equivalent sits, at the front of the clause they
introduce, so there is no new word order to learn. The only two that attach to
the following word are *וְ* and, when a clause follows, the *שֶׁ* of chapter 8.

| | |
|---|---|
| וְ | and |
| אֲבָל | but |
| אוֹ | or |
| כִּי | because |
| לָכֵן | therefore, so |
| אָז | so, then |
| גַּם | also |
| רַק | only |
| בְּעִיקָּר | mainly |
| קוֹדֶם כֹּל | first of all |
| אַחַר כָּךְ | afterwards |
| לְבַסּוֹף | finally |
| לְמָשָׁל | for example |

::: examples
לֹא בָּאתִי כִּי הָיִיתִי חוֹלֶה || I didn't come because I was ill
הָיִיתִי חוֹלֶה, לָכֵן לֹא בָּאתִי || I was ill, so I didn't come
קוֹדֶם כֹּל נֹאכַל, אַחַר כָּךְ נְדַבֵּר || first we'll eat, afterwards we'll talk
גַּם אֲנִי רוֹצֶה || I want some too
רַק הוּא יוֹדֵעַ || only he knows
:::

*גַּם* sits in front of the word it adds: *גַּם אֲנִי* is "me too", *אֲנִי גַּם
רוֹצֶה* is "I also want". Put it next to the thing you mean.

## Because, and the trap in it

::: rule
*בִּגְלַל* /biglal/ takes a noun. *מִפְּנֵי שֶׁ* and *כִּי* take a whole clause.
:::

The distinction is the same one English makes between "because of" and
"because", and English speakers get it right by accident in their own language
and wrong in Hebrew, because *בִּגְלַל* is the word they learned first.

::: examples
בִּגְלַל הַגֶּשֶׁם || because of the rain
בִּגְלַל הַפְּקָקִים אֵיחַרְתִּי || I was late because of the traffic
מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהוּא בָּא || because he came
לֹא יָצָאנוּ כִּי יָרַד גֶּשֶׁם || we didn't go out because it rained
:::

::: warning
Everybody says *בִּגְלַל שֶׁהוּא בָּא*, and the Academy of the Hebrew Language
has been objecting to it for decades. It is the single most complained-about
construction in the language. In speech, say it. In anything you submit or
publish, write *מִפְּנֵי שֶׁ* or *כִּי*.
:::

::: tip
*כִּי* is the safe everyday answer for because. It is correct with a clause,
nobody objects to it, and it is shorter than the alternatives.
:::

::: exercise Join it up
Say "he runs fast" || *הוּא רָץ מַהֵר* || the adjective is used unchanged
Say "she works quietly" || *הִיא עוֹבֶדֶת בְּשֶׁקֶט* || *בְּ* plus an abstract noun
Say "I have never been there" || *אַף פַּעַם לֹא הָיִיתִי שָׁם* || *אַף פַּעַם* requires *לֹא*
Say "I haven't finished yet" || *עוֹד לֹא סִיַּימְתִּי* || *עוֹד* plus *לֹא* is "not yet"
Say "because of the rain" || *בִּגְלַל הַגֶּשֶׁם* || *בִּגְלַל* takes a noun
Say "because he came", carefully || *מִפְּנֵי שֶׁהוּא בָּא* || *בִּגְלַל שֶׁ* is spoken, not written
Say "I was ill, so I didn't come" || *הָיִיתִי חוֹלֶה, לָכֵן לֹא בָּאתִי* || *לָכֵן* introduces the result
Say "me too" || *גַּם אֲנִי* || *גַּם* goes in front of what it adds
:::

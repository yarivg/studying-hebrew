# Comparison and the most

English has two machines for comparison: an ending for short adjectives
(bigger, taller) and a word for long ones (more interesting). Hebrew has one
word and no endings at all. The adjective you learned in chapter 12 is the
adjective you use, unchanged, in every comparison you will ever make.

## More and less

*יוֹתֵר* /yoter/ means more, *פָּחוֹת* /pachot/ means less, and *מִ* attached to
the next word means than.

::: examples
הוּא יוֹתֵר גָּבוֹהַּ מִמֶּנִּי || he is taller than me
הוּא גָּבוֹהַּ יוֹתֵר מִמֶּנִּי || he is taller than me
הַדִּירָה הַזֹּאת יוֹתֵר גְּדוֹלָה מֵהַקּוֹדֶמֶת || this flat is bigger than the previous one
הַסֵּפֶר פָּחוֹת מְעַנְיֵין מֵהַסֶּרֶט || the book is less interesting than the film
יֵשׁ לוֹ יוֹתֵר כֶּסֶף מִמֶּנִּי || he has more money than me
:::

When the thing compared is a noun rather than a pronoun, *מִ* attaches to it
directly and swallows the article: *מֵהַסֶּרֶט*, than the film, *מֵהַבַּיִת
שֶׁלִּי*, than my house. Before a definite noun the *מִ* becomes *מֵ*, which is
the same behaviour you saw with *בְּ* and *לְ* in front of *הַ*.

*יוֹתֵר* can stand before the adjective or after it, and both are ordinary.
Before it is a shade more colloquial, after it a shade more careful. Nothing
else changes: *גָּדוֹל* stays *גָּדוֹל*, and there is no Hebrew word that
behaves like "bigger".

::: rule
Hebrew never modifies the adjective itself. Comparison is *יוֹתֵר* or
*פָּחוֹת* plus the plain adjective, with *מִ* on whatever you are comparing to.
:::

## מִן with an ending

When "than" is followed by a pronoun, the preposition *מִן* /min/ takes the
endings. This set is irregular enough to be worth a table on its own.

| | |
|---|---|
| מִמֶּנִּי | than me |
| מִמְּךָ | than you (m) |
| מִמֵּךְ | than you (f) |
| מִמֶּנּוּ | than him |
| מִמֶּנָּה | than her |
| מֵאִיתָּנוּ | than us |
| מִכֶּם | than you (m pl) |
| מִכֶּן | than you (f pl) |
| מֵהֶם | than them (m) |
| מֵהֶן | than them (f) |

::: warning
*מִמֶּנּוּ* is both "than him" and, in the older grammar, "than us". Modern
Hebrew settles the ambiguity by using *מֵאִיתָּנוּ* for us and leaving
*מִמֶּנּוּ* to him. Use *מֵאִיתָּנוּ* and you will never be misheard.
:::

## The most

::: examples
הוּא הֲכִי גָּבוֹהַּ בַּכִּיתָּה || he is the tallest in the class
זֶה הַסֶּרֶט הֲכִי טוֹב שֶׁרָאִיתִי || that is the best film I have seen
הָאוֹכֶל הֲכִי טָעִים בָּעִיר || the tastiest food in town
זֶה הֲכִי פָּחוֹת מְעַנְיֵין || that is the least interesting
:::

*הֲכִי* /hakhi/ goes in front of the adjective and does the whole job. The noun
keeps its article, the adjective does not need one, and nothing declines. It
also works on adverbs and on *פָּחוֹת*, which is how you say the least of
something without any new vocabulary at all. Where English needs "in" or "of"
to finish the comparison, Hebrew uses *בְּ*: *הֲכִי טוֹב בָּעוֹלָם*, the best in
the world.

The formal alternative puts *בְּיוֹתֵר* after a definite adjective:

::: examples
הַבַּיִת הַגָּדוֹל בְּיוֹתֵר || the largest house
הַהַחְלָטָה הַחֲשׁוּבָה בְּיוֹתֵר || the most important decision
:::

::: rule
*הֲכִי* in speech and in most writing, *הַ...בְּיוֹתֵר* in formal prose. They
mean the same thing.
:::

## The same as

::: examples
הוּא גָּבוֹהַּ כְּמוֹ אָחִיו || he is as tall as his brother
הִיא עוֹבֶדֶת כְּמוֹנִי || she works like me
זֶה אוֹתוֹ דָּבָר || it's the same thing
זֶה בְּדִיּוּק אוֹתָהּ בְּעָיָה || that is exactly the same problem
זֶה כָּל כָּךְ יָפֶה || it is so beautiful
יֵשׁ כָּל כָּךְ הַרְבֵּה אֲנָשִׁים || there are so many people
:::

Equality needs no special construction: the adjective stays flat here too, and
*כְּמוֹ* simply sits between the two things being matched.

*כְּמוֹ* /kmo/ takes the same endings as other prepositions: *כָּמוֹנִי*,
*כָּמוֹךָ*, *כָּמוֹהוּ*. *אוֹתוֹ דָּבָר* is literally "the same thing" and
*אוֹתוֹ* agrees with the noun in front: *אוֹתוֹ סֵפֶר*, *אוֹתָהּ מִסְעָדָה*.

## Too much, enough, much more

::: examples
זֶה יוֹתֵר מִדַּי || that's too much
הַקָּפֶה חַם מִדַּי || the coffee is too hot
יֵשׁ מַסְפִּיק זְמַן || there is enough time
זֶה מַסְפִּיק גָּדוֹל || it is big enough
זֶה הַרְבֵּה יוֹתֵר טוֹב || that is much better
קְצָת יוֹתֵר לְאַט || a little more slowly
:::

::: note
*מִדַּי* follows the adjective (*חַם מִדַּי*) but *יוֹתֵר מִדַּי* comes before a
noun (*יוֹתֵר מִדַּי אֲנָשִׁים*, too many people). *מַסְפִּיק* goes in front of
both. These are word-order habits, not rules with a reason behind them.
:::

::: tip
This is one of the places where Hebrew is genuinely easier than English. No
irregular comparatives, no good/better/best, no deciding between "commoner"
and "more common". *טוֹב* becomes *יוֹתֵר טוֹב* and *הֲכִי טוֹב*, and that is
the entire system.
:::

::: exercise Compare
Say "he is taller than me" || *הוּא יוֹתֵר גָּבוֹהַּ מִמֶּנִּי* || or *גָּבוֹהַּ יוֹתֵר*
Say "she is older than you" (to a woman) || *הִיא יוֹתֵר מְבוּגֶּרֶת מִמֵּךְ* || *מִן* takes the ending
Say "the book is less interesting than the film" || *הַסֵּפֶר פָּחוֹת מְעַנְיֵין מֵהַסֶּרֶט* || *פָּחוֹת* works exactly like *יוֹתֵר*
Say "the biggest house" || *הַבַּיִת הֲכִי גָּדוֹל* || or formally *הַבַּיִת הַגָּדוֹל בְּיוֹתֵר*
Say "he works like me" || *הוּא עוֹבֵד כָּמוֹנִי* || *כְּמוֹ* declines like other prepositions
Say "the coffee is too hot" || *הַקָּפֶה חַם מִדַּי* || *מִדַּי* comes after the adjective
Say "there is enough time" || *יֵשׁ מַסְפִּיק זְמַן* || *מַסְפִּיק* comes before the noun
Why is there no Hebrew word for "bigger"? || the adjective never changes || comparison is carried by *יוֹתֵר*
:::

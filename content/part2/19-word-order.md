# Word order and emphasis

Modern Hebrew is a subject-verb-object language, like English, so most of the
time you can put the words where an English sentence would put them and be
right. What this chapter is about is the other times: what happens when you
move something, and which things cannot move at all.

## The neutral order

::: examples
דָּנִי קָרָא אֶת הַסֵּפֶר || Dani read the book
הַיְלָדִים אוֹכְלִים אֲרוּחַת בּוֹקֶר || the children are eating breakfast
אֲנִי אֶכְתּוֹב לְךָ מָחָר || I will write to you tomorrow
:::

That is the order to use when you have nothing in particular to emphasise,
and the order a sentence returns to after any of the moves below.

## Verb first

Putting the verb before its subject is not wrong and it is not poetic. It is a
normal choice in narrative and in writing, and it does a specific job: it
introduces something new into the story rather than saying something about
something already in it.

::: examples
בָּא אִישׁ וְשָׁאַל אֵיפֹה הַתַּחֲנָה || a man came up and asked where the station was
נִכְנְסָה אִישָּׁה לַחֶדֶר || a woman came into the room
קָרָה מַשֶּׁהוּ מוּזָר || something strange happened
:::

::: rule
Verb before subject reads as "and then". It is the order of a story moving
forward, and it goes with an indefinite subject far more often than with a
definite one.
:::

*נִכְנְסָה אִישָּׁה לַחֶדֶר* is a woman entering the room for the first time.
*הָאִישָּׁה נִכְנְסָה לַחֶדֶר* is the woman we already know about, and now here
is what she did. English does the same work with "a" and "the", and Hebrew
does it with the order as well.

## Moving the object to the front

Here is where *אֶת* earns its keep. A definite object carries its marker
wherever it goes, so the listener can tell it is the object even when it is
sitting in the subject's chair.

::: examples
קָרָאתִי אֶת הַסֵּפֶר הַזֶּה כְּבָר || I have already read this book
אֶת הַסֵּפֶר הַזֶּה קָרָאתִי כְּבָר || THIS book I have already read
אֶת דָּנִי לֹא רָאִיתִי הַיּוֹם || Dani I have not seen today
:::

::: rule
*אֶת* is what makes fronting safe. English cannot front an object without
sounding strange, because nothing marks it. Hebrew can, because the marker
travels with the word.
:::

The meaning does not change. The object is simply under a spotlight: this
book as opposed to some other one.

## Topic first, pronoun left behind

There is a second way to front, and it is the one you will hear in speech.
Lift the noun out of the sentence, put it at the front with a pause, and leave
a pronoun where it used to be.

::: examples
הַסֵּפֶר הַזֶּה, קָרָאתִי אוֹתוֹ || this book, I read it
דָּנִי, לֹא רָאִיתִי אוֹתוֹ הַיּוֹם || Dani, I have not seen him today
הַדִּירָה הַחֲדָשָׁה, הִיא מָצְאָה אוֹתָהּ בְּאִינְטֶרְנֶט || the new flat, she found it online
:::

Note that the fronted noun has no *אֶת* on it. It is not the object any more;
the pronoun is. This is the same resumptive pronoun you met in chapter 8 with
*שֶׁ*, doing the same job in a different place.

## What cannot move

A short list, and it is not negotiable.

| Element | Position | Example |
|---|---|---|
| adjective | after its noun | *בַּיִת גָּדוֹל* |
| number | before its noun | *שְׁלוֹשָׁה סְפָרִים* |
| *אֶחָד* | after its noun | *סֵפֶר אֶחָד* |
| *שֶׁל* phrase | after its noun | *הַסֵּפֶר שֶׁלִּי* |
| question word | first in the clause | *מָתַי אַתָּה בָּא?* |
| *לֹא* | immediately before what it negates | *אֲנִי לֹא קָרָאתִי* |

*אֶחָד* is the one number that behaves like an adjective instead of a number,
which is why it follows. Everything else counts from in front.

*לֹא* is worth its own look, because it is the cheapest emphasis in the
language. Move it and you move the denial.

::: examples
אֲנִי לֹא קָרָאתִי אֶת הַסֵּפֶר || I did not read the book
לֹא אֲנִי קָרָאתִי אֶת הַסֵּפֶר || it was not I who read the book
לֹא אֶת הַסֵּפֶר קָרָאתִי || it was not the book that I read
:::

## Adverbs go where you want them

Time and manner adverbs are genuinely free, and the position changes the
weight rather than the meaning.

::: examples
אֶתְמוֹל הָלַכְתִּי לַיָּם || yesterday, I went to the sea
הָלַכְתִּי אֶתְמוֹל לַיָּם || I went to the sea yesterday
הָלַכְתִּי לַיָּם אֶתְמוֹל || I went to the sea, yesterday
:::

Front position sets the scene: the sentence is about yesterday. Final position
is the neutral one, an added detail. The middle is the least common of the
three and sounds slightly formal.

The small adverbs from chapter 10 are less free. *כְּבָר*, *עֲדַיִין*,
*תָּמִיד* and *רַק* sit immediately in front of whatever they apply to.

::: examples
אֲנִי תָּמִיד קָם מוּקְדָּם || I always get up early
רַק דָּנִי יוֹדֵעַ || only Dani knows
דָּנִי יוֹדֵעַ רַק אֶת זֶה || Dani only knows this
:::

## Why Hebrew can afford this

English leans on word order because it has almost nothing else. "The dog bit
the man" and "the man bit the dog" differ only in the order. Hebrew has two
extra sources of information, and they do not care where the words are.

::: rule
*אֶת* says which noun is the object. The verb ending says which person is the
subject. With both of those working, word order is left free to do something
else: emphasis.
:::

::: examples
הַכֶּלֶב נָשַׁךְ אֶת הָאִישׁ || the dog bit the man
אֶת הָאִישׁ נָשַׁךְ הַכֶּלֶב || the man, the dog bit
כָּתַבְתִּי מִכְתָּב || I wrote a letter
:::

The second sentence has the object first and the subject last, and still only
one reading: the dog did the biting. And *כָּתַבְתִּי* needs no *אֲנִי* at
all, because the *תִּי* already said it.

::: exercise Move it and say what changed
Put "Dani read the book" in neutral order || *דָּנִי קָרָא אֶת הַסֵּפֶר* || subject, verb, object
Front the object of that sentence || *אֶת הַסֵּפֶר קָרָא דָּנִי* || the *אֶת* travels with the object
Why can Hebrew do that and English cannot? || English has no object marker, so the fronted noun would read as the subject || *אֶת* keeps the roles clear
Topicalise *הַסֵּפֶר הַזֶּה* with a resumptive pronoun || *הַסֵּפֶר הַזֶּה, קָרָאתִי אוֹתוֹ* || no *אֶת* on the fronted noun
Where does *אֶחָד* go? || after the noun || *סֵפֶר אֶחָד*, unlike every other number
What is the difference between *אֲנִי לֹא קָרָאתִי* and *לֹא אֲנִי קָרָאתִי*? || the first denies the reading, the second denies that it was me || *לֹא* negates whatever follows it
Rewrite *אִישָּׁה נִכְנְסָה לַחֶדֶר* in narrative order || *נִכְנְסָה אִישָּׁה לַחֶדֶר* || verb first, with the flavour of "and then"
Where can *אֶתְמוֹל* go? || front, middle or end || all three are correct; the front sets the scene
:::

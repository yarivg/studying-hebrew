# Making adjectives out of nouns

Hebrew has one ending that turns almost any noun into an adjective meaning
"of that thing", "to do with that thing". It is a single yod, *ִי*, and it is
the most productive suffix in the modern language. Learn it and several
hundred words come with it.

## The ending

The grammarians call it the *יַחַס* /yakhas/ ending, the relation ending, and
it is borrowed from Arabic grammar under the name nisba. Take the noun, strip
a feminine *ָה* if there is one, and add *ִי*.

| Noun | Adjective | |
|---|---|---|
| יִשְׂרָאֵל | יִשְׂרְאֵלִי | Israeli |
| אָבִיב | אֲבִיבִי | spring-like, vernal |
| חוֹרֶף | חוֹרְפִּי | wintry |
| יָם | יַמִּי | marine, sea- |
| מִשְׁפָּחָה | מִשְׁפַּחְתִּי | family, familial |
| עִיר | עִירוֹנִי | urban, municipal |
| כְּלָל | כְּלָלִי | general |
| שָׁנָה | שְׁנָתִי | annual |
| מֶרְכָּז | מֶרְכָּזִי | central |
| כַּלְכָּלָה | כַּלְכָּלִי | economic |

Two things happen to the stem on the way. The vowels reduce, as they do in
smikhut: *יִשְׂרָאֵל* becomes *יִשְׂרְאֵ־*, *חוֹרֶף* becomes *חוֹרְפּ־*. And a
noun whose feminine ending was *ָה* brings back the older *ת*:
*מִשְׁפָּחָה* gives *מִשְׁפַּחְתִּי*, *שָׁנָה* gives *שְׁנָתִי*, *חֶבְרָה*
gives *חֶבְרָתִי* /khevrati/, social.

## Its four forms

It inflects like any other adjective, and it follows its noun and agrees with
it in the usual three ways.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| masculine | יִשְׂרְאֵלִי | יִשְׂרְאֵלִים |
| feminine | יִשְׂרְאֵלִית | יִשְׂרְאֵלִיּוֹת |

::: examples
סֶרֶט יִשְׂרְאֵלִי || an Israeli film
חֶבְרָה יִשְׂרְאֵלִית || an Israeli company
סְטוּדֶנְטִים יִשְׂרְאֵלִים || Israeli students
מִשְׁפָּחוֹת יִשְׂרְאֵלִיּוֹת || Israeli families
:::

::: note
In careful writing the masculine plural of a relation adjective is spelled
with two yods when it describes a thing rather than a person:
*מוּצָרִים יִשְׂרְאֵלִיִּים*, Israeli products, against *יִשְׂרְאֵלִים*, the
people. Newspapers apply this inconsistently and most speakers never hear the
difference. *הִיסְטוֹרִיִּים*, *מִקְצוֹעִיִּים* and the rest behave the same
way.
:::

::: note
A relation adjective doubles as a noun with no change at all. *הוּא
יִשְׂרְאֵלִי* is both "he is Israeli" and "he is an Israeli". The same for
*צָרְפָתִי* /tzarfati/, *רוּסִי*, *דָּתִי* /dati/, religious, and a religious
person.
:::

## Where it is productive

Three areas where the ending is still generating new words every year.

- **Nationalities and origins.** *צָרְפָתִי*, *רוּסִי*, *גֶּרְמָנִי*,
  *סִינִי*, *אִיטַלְקִי*, *מָרוֹקָאִי*.
- **Fields of study and specialism.** *הִיסְטוֹרִי* /histori/,
  *מָתֵימָטִי*, *מוּזִיקָלִי*, *פִּילוֹסוֹפִי*, *כִּימִי*. These are borrowed
  words with a Hebrew ending glued on, and the glue holds.
- **Anything new.** *רִשְׁתִּי* from *רֶשֶׁת*, network; *סְבִיבָתִי* from
  *סְבִיבָה*, environmental; *מְקוּוָּן* is a different formation but the
  impulse is the same. If a noun exists, Hebrew will build the adjective when
  it needs one and nobody will find it strange.

There are two extended versions of the ending worth recognising: *וֹנִי* as
in *עִירוֹנִי*, *רִאשׁוֹנִי* /rishoni/, primary, and *ָנִי* as in *רוּחָנִי*
/rukhani/, spiritual, and *עַצְבָּנִי* /atzbani/, irritable.

## The noun of quality

The other side of the same coin is *וּת*, which makes an abstract noun out of
almost anything: the quality of being whatever the word says.

::: examples
יֶלֶד becomes יַלְדוּת || a child, childhood
חֵירוּת || freedom: the ending on an old root with no modern noun beside it
סַבְלָן becomes סַבְלָנוּת || patient, patience
עַצְמָאִי becomes עַצְמָאוּת || independent, independence
אַחֲרַאי becomes אַחְרָיוּת || responsible, responsibility
:::

::: rule
*ִי* makes an adjective out of a noun. *וּת* makes a noun out of an adjective.
Run both and you get the quality of belonging to something.
:::

That stacking is real and common: *אֱנוֹשׁ* gives *אֱנוֹשִׁי*, human, which
gives *אֱנוֹשִׁיּוּת*, humanity. *יִשְׂרְאֵלִי* gives *יִשְׂרְאֵלִיּוּת*,
Israeliness, a word you will see in a newspaper column any week of the year.
*מִקְצוֹעִי* gives *מִקְצוֹעִיּוּת*, professionalism. Note the doubled yod:
the *וּת* is added to the full adjective, yod and all.

## The other adjective shapes

Not every adjective is built this way. Four mishkalim account for most of the
rest, and recognising them helps you guess a meaning and a feminine form.

| Shape | Examples | |
|---|---|---|
| .ָ.ִי. | עָשִׁיר, צָעִיר, בָּהִיר, נָעִים | rich, young, bright, pleasant |
| .ָ.וּ. | עָצוּב, שָׁמוּר, סָגוּר, בָּרוּר | sad, kept, closed, clear |
| .ָ.וֹ. | גָּדוֹל, גָּבוֹהַּ, עָמוֹק, קָרוֹב | big, tall, deep, near |
| .ָ.ָ. | חָכָם, חָזָק, יָשָׁן, חָדָשׁ | clever, strong, old, new |

The *.ָ.וּ.* group is not really a separate shape at all: it is the passive
participle of pa'al, the form chapter 1 said pa'al keeps for itself.
*סָגוּר* is literally "having been closed". And the present participles of the
other binyanim do adjective duty in exactly the same way: *מְעַנְיֵין*,
*מְסוּדָּר*, *מַתְאִים* /matim/, suitable.

::: warning
A relation adjective is not always the natural translation of an English noun
used as a modifier. "A history book" is *סֵפֶר הִיסְטוֹרְיָה*, not
*סֵפֶר הִיסְטוֹרִי*, which would mean a book that is itself historic. "A
coffee cup" is *סֵפֶל קָפֶה*. English piles nouns in front of nouns; Hebrew
usually answers with smikhut, and only reaches for *ִי* when the meaning is
genuinely "having the character of". Chapter 7 is about the alternative.
:::

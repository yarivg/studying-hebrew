# Contrast and concession

English has one word, "but", doing three different jobs. Hebrew splits the
work, and the split is the clearest single marker of a good speaker. An
Israeli will not correct you for saying *אֲבָל* where *אֶלָּא* belongs, but
they will hear it.

## But, and but rather

*אֲבָל* /aval/ is the ordinary "but". It joins two statements that both stand,
and the second one qualifies the first.

::: examples
רָצִיתִי לָלֶכֶת אֲבָל לֹא הָיָה לִי זְמַן || I wanted to go but I did not have time
הַמִּסְעָדָה יְקָרָה אֲבָל הָאוֹכֶל מְצוּיָן || the restaurant is expensive but the food is excellent
לֹא רָצִיתִי לָלֶכֶת אֲבָל הָלַכְתִּי || I did not want to go but I went
:::

*אֶלָּא* /ela/ is a different animal. It does not qualify anything. It
replaces something the first clause has just denied, and it can only appear
after a negative.

::: examples
לֹא רָצִיתִי לָלֶכֶת אֶלָּא לְהִישָּׁאֵר || I did not want to go but to stay
הוּא לֹא מוֹרֶה אֶלָּא רוֹפֵא || he is not a teacher but a doctor
זֶה לֹא קָרָה אֶתְמוֹל אֶלָּא לִפְנֵי שָׁבוּעַ || it did not happen yesterday but a week ago
לֹא בִּיקַּשְׁתִּי כֶּסֶף אֶלָּא עֵצָה || I did not ask for money but for advice
:::

::: rule
*אֶלָּא* corrects. *אֲבָל* concedes. If the second half supplies the true
version of what the first half denied, it is *אֶלָּא*; if both halves are true
and merely pull against each other, it is *אֲבָל*.
:::

Look at the third and fourth examples of the first block against the first of
the second. *לֹא רָצִיתִי לָלֶכֶת אֲבָל הָלַכְתִּי* has a negative in it and
still takes *אֲבָל*, because going is not a correction of not wanting to go:
both things happened. *לֹא רָצִיתִי לָלֶכֶת אֶלָּא לְהִישָּׁאֵר* replaces
*לָלֶכֶת* with *לְהִישָּׁאֵר*, and that is the test.

::: warning
*אֶלָּא* after a positive clause is simply ungrammatical. *רָצִיתִי לָלֶכֶת
אֶלָּא לֹא הָיָה זְמַן* is not a sentence. There must be a *לֹא* or an *אֵין*
in front of it.
:::

The construction *לֹא רַק ... אֶלָּא גַּם* is the same logic frozen into a
frame, and it is common at every register.

::: examples
הוּא לֹא רַק מְדַבֵּר עִבְרִית אֶלָּא גַּם כּוֹתֵב || he not only speaks Hebrew but also writes it
זֶה לֹא רַק יָקָר אֶלָּא גַּם לֹא טוֹב || it is not only expensive but also not good
:::

::: note
*אֶלָּא שֶׁ* is a separate item meaning "except that": *הַכֹּל הָיָה מוּכָן,
אֶלָּא שֶׁאִישׁ לֹא הִגִּיעַ*, everything was ready, except that nobody came.
And *אֶלָּא אִם כֵּן* is "unless", from chapter 4.
:::

## Although

Concession is the other half of contrast: you grant the first thing and the
second happens anyway. The same noun-against-clause split as in chapter 3
applies.

| Connector | Takes | Register |
|---|---|---|
| לַמְרוֹת | a noun | neutral |
| לַמְרוֹת שֶׁ | a clause | neutral, very common |
| אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁ | a clause | careful, written |
| עַל אַף | a noun | written |
| חֵרֶף | a noun | formal, literary |

::: examples
לַמְרוֹת הַגֶּשֶׁם יָצָאנוּ לְטִיּוּל || despite the rain we went out for a walk
לַמְרוֹת שֶׁיָּרַד גֶּשֶׁם יָצָאנוּ לְטִיּוּל || although it was raining we went out for a walk
אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁהוּא צָעִיר, הוּא מְנוּסֶּה מְאוֹד || although he is young, he is very experienced
חֵרֶף הַקְּשָׁיִים הַפְּרוֹיֶקְט הִסְתַּיֵּים בַּזְּמַן || despite the difficulties the project finished on time
:::

::: note
In unpointed text *אַף עַל פִּי שֶׁ* is regularly abbreviated to אעפ"י, and
*לַמְרוֹת* is sometimes mis-analysed by learners as a verb. It is not: it is a
preposition, and *לַמְרוֹת שֶׁ*, unlike *בִּגְלַל שֶׁ*, is fully accepted.
:::

## Nevertheless

When the concession stands in its own sentence, Hebrew has a small set of
adverbs for the turn.

::: examples
יָרַד גֶּשֶׁם. וּבְכָל זֹאת יָצָאנוּ || it rained. We went out all the same
הוּא לֹא לָמַד, וְאַף עַל פִּי כֵן עָבַר אֶת הַבְּחִינָה || he did not study, and nevertheless he passed the exam
לַמְרוֹת הַכֹּל, הִיא לֹא וִיתְּרָה || in spite of everything, she did not give up
:::

## Two things side by side

The last group does not concede anything. It sets two facts in parallel so the
reader can compare them.

::: examples
הוּא עָבַד כָּל הַלַּיְלָה, בְּעוֹד שֶׁהִיא יָשְׁנָה || he worked all night, while she slept
בַּצָּפוֹן יוֹרֵד גֶּשֶׁם. לְעוּמַת זֹאת, בַּדָּרוֹם חַם וְיָבֵשׁ || it is raining in the north. In the south, by contrast, it is hot and dry
הַמְּחִירִים כָּאן גְּבוֹהִים לְעוּמַת שְׁאָר הָאֵזוֹר || prices here are high compared to the rest of the region
מִצַּד אֶחָד זֶה זוֹל, מִצַּד שֵׁנִי זֶה רָחוֹק || on the one hand it is cheap, on the other hand it is far
:::

*לְעוּמַת* /le'umat/ takes a noun and means "compared with". *לְעוּמַת זֹאת*
is the sentence adverb built on it, "by contrast", and it opens a sentence.
*בְּעוֹד שֶׁ* takes a clause.

::: tip
Three questions, in order. Am I correcting what I just denied? *אֶלָּא*. Am I
granting something and going ahead anyway? *לַמְרוֹת שֶׁ*. Am I just holding
two facts up next to each other? *בְּעוֹד שֶׁ* or *לְעוּמַת זֹאת*. If none of
them fits, it was *אֲבָל* all along.
:::

::: exercise But, but rather, although
"I did not want to go but to stay" || *לֹא רָצִיתִי לָלֶכֶת אֶלָּא לְהִישָּׁאֵר* || the second half corrects the first
"I did not want to go but I went" || *לֹא רָצִיתִי לָלֶכֶת אֲבָל הָלַכְתִּי* || nothing is being corrected, so *אֲבָל*
"He is not a teacher but a doctor" || *הוּא לֹא מוֹרֶה אֶלָּא רוֹפֵא* || a straight replacement
Why can *אֶלָּא* not follow *רָצִיתִי*? || it needs a negative in front of it || no *לֹא*, no *אֶלָּא*
"despite the rain" || *לַמְרוֹת הַגֶּשֶׁם* || a noun, so no *שֶׁ*
"although it rained" || *לַמְרוֹת שֶׁיָּרַד גֶּשֶׁם* || a clause, so *שֶׁ*
"not only cheap but also fast" || *לֹא רַק זוֹל אֶלָּא גַּם מָהִיר* || the frozen *לֹא רַק ... אֶלָּא גַּם* frame
"on the one hand ... on the other hand" || *מִצַּד אֶחָד ... מִצַּד שֵׁנִי* || two facts held side by side
:::

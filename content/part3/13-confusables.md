# Pairs that catch everyone

Some mistakes are private to one learner. These are not. Every English speaker
learning Hebrew makes the sixteen mistakes in this chapter, usually for
several years, and the reason is almost always the same: English covers two
Hebrew jobs with one word, or Hebrew covers two English words with one sound.

Nothing here is difficult. It is all a matter of noticing.

## Same meaning, different shelf

**לָמָּה and מַדּוּעַ.** Both mean "why". *לָמָּה* /lama/ is what people say.
*מַדּוּעַ* /madu'a/ is what forms, editorials and school exercises say. There
is no difference in meaning at all, only in height.

::: examples
לָמָּה אַתָּה צוֹחֵק? || why are you laughing?
מַדּוּעַ לֹא הוֹדִיעוּ לָנוּ מֵרֹאשׁ? || why were we not informed in advance?
:::

**רַק and בִּלְבַד.** Both mean "only". *רַק* goes in front of the thing it
limits and is neutral. *בִּלְבַד* /bilvad/ goes after it and belongs to signs,
contracts and notices.

::: examples
רַק שְׁתֵּי דַּקּוֹת, אֲנִי מַבְטִיחַ. || only two minutes, I promise
הַכְּנִיסָה לַחֲבֵרִים בִּלְבַד. || entry for members only
:::

**קְצָת and מְעַט.** Both mean "a little". *קְצָת* /ktzat/ is the spoken one
and goes with anything. *מְעַט* /me'at/ is written, and it also does the work
of "few" with things you can count.

::: examples
תֵּן לִי קְצָת מַיִם. || give me a bit of water
מְעַט מְאוֹד אֲנָשִׁים יוֹדְעִים אֶת זֶה. || very few people know that
:::

## Same sound, different word

**אִם and עִם.** Not the same spelling: one has an alef and one has an ayin.
But Israelis pronounce both gutturals as nothing at all, so the two words are
exact homophones, and a learner who writes by ear picks wrong about half the
time. *אִם* is "if". *עִם* is "with". Nothing else will tell you apart; you
have to know which letter the word owns.

::: examples
אִם יֵשׁ זְמַן, נֵלֵךְ לַיָּם. || if there's time, we'll go to the sea
בָּאתִי עִם חָבֵר. || I came with a friend
:::

**שָׁנָה and שֵׁינָה.** A year and sleep, one vowel apart. Full spelling just
about keeps them separate, שנה against שינה, but a single yod is all that is
doing it, and any pointed book, which uses defective spelling, writes both as
שנה. In speech there is nothing between them but the vowel.

::: examples
לָמַדְתִּי שָׁנָה בְּתֵל אָבִיב. || I studied for a year in Tel Aviv
חָסְרָה לִי שֵׁינָה. || I'm short of sleep
:::

**דּוֹד and דּוּד.** An uncle and a hot-water boiler, both written דוד, and so
is the name David. This is a real problem and not a joke: *הַדּוּד
הִתְקַלְקֵל* is the single most common sentence in Israeli domestic life.

::: examples
הַדּוֹד שֶׁלִּי גָּר בְּחֵיפָה. || my uncle lives in Haifa
הַדּוּד הִתְקַלְקֵל, אֵין מַיִם חַמִּים. || the boiler has broken, there's no hot water
:::

**The gutturals.** Israeli Hebrew merged ע with א and ח with כ, so pairs that
were once clearly different now sound alike. The danger is in writing, not in
hearing.

::: examples
כָּתַבְתִּי מִכְתָּב. || I wrote a letter
קָטַפְתִּי תַּפּוּחַ מֵהָעֵץ. || I picked an apple off the tree
הוּא עוֹלֶה חָדָשׁ מִצָּרְפַת. || he is a new immigrant from France
מָה עוֹלַלְתָּ לַמִּטְבָּח? || what have you done to the kitchen?
:::

::: warning
*עוֹלֶה* is the present of *לַעֲלוֹת*, to go up, and also the noun for an
immigrant to Israel. *עוֹלֵל* is an infant, and *לְעוֹלֵל* is to perpetrate,
used only of damage and mischief. One vowel apart, and one of them will get
you a strange look at passport control.
:::

## One English word, two Hebrew ones

**יָכוֹל and יוֹדֵעַ.** English "can" covers both being able and knowing how.
Hebrew does not. Knowing how to do something is *יוֹדֵעַ*.

::: examples
אֲנִי יוֹדֵעַ לִשְׂחוֹת. || I can swim, meaning I know how
אֲנִי יָכוֹל לָבוֹא מָחָר. || I can come tomorrow, meaning I am free to
:::

**לָדַעַת and לְהַכִּיר.** Both are "to know". Facts, languages and answers
take *יוֹדֵעַ*. People, cities and books take *מַכִּיר*.

::: examples
אֲנִי יוֹדֵעַ אֶת הַתְּשׁוּבָה. || I know the answer
אֲנִי מַכִּיר אֶת הָאָח שֶׁלּוֹ. || I know his brother
:::

::: warning
*אֲנִי יוֹדֵעַ אוֹתוֹ* about a person is wrong and marks you as a foreigner
immediately. So does *אֲנִי מַכִּיר אֶת הַתְּשׁוּבָה*. Fact or person: pick
the verb from that, every time.
:::

**הָיָה and הָיָה לוֹ.** The same verb, two sentences. Add a *לְ* and "was"
becomes "had", and the verb stops agreeing with the person and starts agreeing
with the thing owned.

::: examples
הוּא הָיָה בַּבַּיִת. || he was at home
הָיְתָה לוֹ מְכוֹנִית. || he had a car
:::

**מְאוֹד and הַרְבֵּה.** *מְאוֹד* is degree and follows what it strengthens.
*הַרְבֵּה* is quantity.

::: examples
אֲנִי אוֹהֵב אוֹתָךְ מְאוֹד. || I love you very much
אֲנִי עוֹבֵד הַרְבֵּה הַשָּׁבוּעַ. || I'm working a lot this week
:::

**כָּל and הַכּוֹל.** *כָּל* needs a noun behind it. *הַכּוֹל* stands alone
and means "everything".

::: examples
כָּל הַיְּלָדִים בַּבַּיִת. || all the children are at home
הַכּוֹל בְּסֵדֶר, אַל תִּדְאַג. || everything is fine, don't worry
:::

**אֵין and לֹא.** *אֵין* negates existence and possession. *לֹא* negates a
verb or a predicate.

::: examples
אֵין לִי זְמַן. || I have no time
לֹא הָלַכְתִּי לָעֲבוֹדָה. || I didn't go to work
:::

**עוֹד and עֲדַיִין.** *עוֹד* is another one, more of something. *עֲדַיִין* is
"still". They overlap in the negative, where *עוֹד לֹא* and *עֲדַיִין לֹא*
both mean "not yet", and speech prefers the first.

::: examples
אֲנִי רוֹצֶה עוֹד קָפֶה. || I want another coffee
הוּא עֲדַיִין לֹא הִגִּיעַ. || he still hasn't arrived
:::

**אֲבָל and אֶלָּא.** *אֲבָל* is the ordinary "but". *אֶלָּא* is "but rather",
and it can only appear after a negative, replacing the thing that was denied.
Chapter 5 of this part has the full treatment.

::: examples
רָצִיתִי לָבוֹא, אֲבָל לֹא יָכוֹלְתִּי. || I wanted to come, but I couldn't
זֶה לֹא כָּחוֹל אֶלָּא יָרוֹק. || it isn't blue but green
:::

**בִּגְלַל and בִּשְׁבִיל.** Cause against benefit. *בִּגְלַל* says what made
it happen, usually something unwelcome. *בִּשְׁבִיל* says who it was for.

::: examples
אִיחַרְתִּי בִּגְלַל הַגֶּשֶׁם. || I was late because of the rain
קָנִיתִי אֶת זֶה בִּשְׁבִילְךָ. || I bought this for you
:::

::: rule
When two Hebrew words translate one English word, the split is almost never
about politeness. It is about a distinction English stopped making: able
against skilled, fact against acquaintance, cause against purpose.
:::

::: tip
Most of these will not be fixed by rereading this chapter. They get fixed by
volume: another hundred pages read and another hundred conversations had. Each
correct use you meet is worth more than the rule, because the rule tells you
which word and the reading tells you how often.
:::

::: exercise Pick the right one
"Why were we not informed" in a formal letter: *לָמָּה* or *מַדּוּעַ*? || *מַדּוּעַ* || same meaning, higher register
"I came ___ a friend" || *עִם* || *אִם* with an alef is "if"
"I can swim" || *אֲנִי יוֹדֵעַ לִשְׂחוֹת* || knowing how takes *יוֹדֵעַ*
"I know his sister" || *אֲנִי מַכִּיר אֶת הָאָחוֹת שֶׁלּוֹ* || people take *לְהַכִּיר*
"He had a car" || *הָיְתָה לוֹ מְכוֹנִית* || the verb agrees with the car, not with him
"Everything is fine" || *הַכּוֹל בְּסֵדֶר* || *כָּל* would need a noun after it
"I have no time" || *אֵין לִי זְמַן* || *לֹא* negates verbs, not existence
What are the two meanings of דוד? || an uncle, and a hot-water boiler || *דּוֹד* and *דּוּד*, and the name David as well
:::

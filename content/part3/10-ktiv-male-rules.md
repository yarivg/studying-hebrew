# The spelling rules

Everything you read in Israel is written in *כְּתִיב מָלֵא* /ktiv male/, full
spelling: no points, and extra vavs and yods put in to carry the vowels the
points would have carried. Chapter 5 of Part I gave you four of the rules
roughly. This chapter gives you the set properly, because at this level you
are writing Hebrew as well as reading it, and the difference between תקווה and
תקוה is the difference between a native hand and a foreign one.

The rules come from the Academy of the Hebrew Language, which publishes them
as *כְּלָלֵי הַכְּתִיב חֲסַר הַנִּיקּוּד*, the rules for spelling without
points. They are prescriptive, they are widely followed, and they are not
followed everywhere.

## The six rules that matter

**1. Every o and every u is written with a ו.**

That covers cholam, shuruk, kubutz and the short kamatz. *סֻכָּר* is סוכר,
*כֻּלָּם* is כולם, *חָכְמָה* is חוכמה, *צָהֳרַיִם* is צוהריים.

The exceptions are words that already have an alef doing the job: לא, זאת,
ראש. And *כָּל* is written כל, on its own and in *כָּל הַיּוֹם*, although the
standalone *הַכּוֹל* is written הכול with the vav. That one pair is worth
memorising, because it is the commonest exception in the language.

**2. Every i is written with a י, except before a doubled letter.**

*עִיר* is עיר, *מִיץ* is מיץ, *אִישׁ* is איש. The exception is the reason the
classic rule gives מלה and כתה and ספר for *מִלָּה*, *כִּתָּה* and *סִפֵּר*:
the lamed, the tav and the pe carry a dagesh chazak, so no yod. In 2017 the
Academy revised this and let the yod back in, so the current forms are מילה,
כיתה, סיפר, and this course writes them that way. Older books, and plenty of
newer ones, do not.

**3. A tzere does not gain a yod it never had.**

*שֵׁם* is שם, *בֵּן* is בן, *לֵב* is לב, *חֵן* is חן. If the pointed word has
a yod, it stays: *בֵּית סֵפֶר* is בית ספר, with a yod in the first word and
none in the second. Segol and patach and kamatz gadol are never written at
all. This is why unpointed Hebrew still has to be read in context: the a and
the e are simply not on the page.

**4. A consonantal v in the middle of a word is written וו.**

One vav would be read as a vowel. Doubling it says "this is the consonant".
*מִצְוָוה* is מצווה, *תִּקְוָוה* is תקווה, *אֲוִויר* is אוויר. A prefix can
push a vav into the middle of a word and trigger the doubling:
*וַעֲדָה* is ועדה, but *הַוַּועֲדָה* is הוועדה.

**5. A consonantal y between two vowels is written יי.**

*בִּנְיָינִים* is בניינים, *עִנְיָין* is עניין, *שְׁנִיָּיה* is שנייה,
*עֲלִיָּיה* is עלייה. The same doubling marks the "my" ending on a plural
preposition, which is how עליי (on me) stays distinct from עליו (on him).

**6. Neither doubling happens at the start or the end of a word.**

There is nothing to confuse the letter with in those positions. *וָרוֹד* is
ורוד, *וִילוֹן* is וילון, *וִיכּוּחַ* is ויכוח, all with one vav. At the end:
עכשיו, קו, תו, and the final yod in גוי, חי, בנוי.

::: warning
Borrowed words ignore rule 6 at the start. Israelis write וואטסאפ, וויסקי and
וואלה with two vavs, because a single vav at the front of a foreign word gets
read as "and". The rule says one; the country writes two; write two.
:::

## Where the rules run out

A rule set that has been revised twice leaves a generation of print behind it,
so several common words have two spellings in live use and neither is wrong.

| Seen as | And as | |
|---|---|---|
| אמא | אימא | mum |
| מלה | מילה | word |
| ספור | סיפור | story |
| דוגמא | דוגמה | example |
| שמים | שמיים | sky |

Proper names do as they please: someone spells their own name the way their
family spells it, and there is no arguing with it. Loanwords have only usage
to go on. And the Academy's rules say nothing about acronyms, which is why
you will meet both צה"ל and צהל in the wild.

::: rule
Where two spellings are current, a dictionary lists both. Pick one, be
consistent inside a document, and do not correct an Israeli.
:::

## Full spelling and nikud together

Traditionally the two do not mix. A text that carries points does not need the
extra letters, so a Tanakh, a siddur, a book of poetry and a children's book
are all pointed and defective at once: שֻׁלְחָן with a kubutz, מִצְוָה with
one vav.

This course does the opposite, deliberately. Every Hebrew word here is in full
spelling with the points sitting on top: *שׁוּלְחָן*, *מִצְוָוה*,
*בִּנְיָינִים*. The reason is the nikud toggle. Switch the points off and what
is left is exactly what you will meet on a sign, in a newspaper or in a text
message. With defective spelling you would be left with שלחן and מצוה, which
nobody writes, and you would have learned a skeleton you can never use.

::: note
The cost of this choice is that the pointed forms in this course are not
identical to the pointed forms in a printed prayer book. When you open one and
find שֻׁלְחָן, nothing has gone wrong: that is the same word in the other
convention.
:::

::: tip
The practical consequence, and the reason this chapter exists: when you look a
word up and the dictionary does not have it, add a ו or a י, or take one away,
and look again. Nine times out of ten the word is there under the other
spelling. This applies to search boxes, to app dictionaries and to the index
of a book.
:::

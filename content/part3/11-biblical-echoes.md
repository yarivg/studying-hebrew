# Biblical Hebrew in modern speech

Modern Hebrew was rebuilt out of a written language that had been read and not
spoken for a very long time, and the seams show. An Israeli newsreader will
use a particle from Isaiah in one sentence and Arabic slang in the next, and
neither sounds odd to anyone. This chapter is about the older layer: enough of
it to read a verse, and enough to recognise the pieces of it that are lying
around in ordinary speech.

You are not being asked to learn Biblical Hebrew. You are being asked to stop
being surprised by it.

## The vav that flips the tense

Biblical Hebrew did not have a past and a future in the way you learned them
in Part II. It had two forms, and the narrative past was built by putting a
*וַ* with a dagesh in front of what looks like a future verb.

| Looks like | Actually means |
|---|---|
| *וַיֹּאמֶר* | and he said |
| *וַתֹּאמֶר* | and she said |
| *וַיֵּלֶךְ* | and he went |
| *וַיְהִי* | and it came to pass |

Take *יֹאמַר*, he will say. Put *וַ* on the front and it becomes *וַיֹּאמֶר*,
he said. This is why the book of Genesis is a chain of words beginning with
ו: the vav is not really "and", it is the tense marker.

::: examples
וַיֹּאמֶר אֱלוֹהִים: יְהִי אוֹר. וַיְהִי אוֹר. || And God said: let there be light. And there was light.
וַיֹּאמֶר ה' אֶל אַבְרָם: לֶךְ לְךָ מֵאַרְצְךָ. || And the Lord said to Abram: go from your land.
:::

*יְהִי* is a third form, the jussive, "let it be". It survives in *יְהִי
רָצוֹן*, "may it be Your will", which opens a great many blessings.

The trick works in reverse too. A *וְ* on a past-tense verb throws it into the
future: *וְהָיָה בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא*, "and it shall be on that day", is a past
tense form meaning a future. You do not need to produce any of this. You need
to know that a verse full of past-looking verbs may be prophecy, and a verse
full of future-looking verbs is probably a story.

::: note
Two vav-consecutive forms are still in live use. *וַיְהִי מָה* means "come
what may", and *וְנַהֲפוֹךְ הוּא* means "quite the opposite", and educated
speakers use both without a hint of quotation marks.
:::

## Frozen particles

These are single words from the old language that survive inside phrases, in
formal writing, or both. Most of them you will only ever read.

| Word | Meaning | Where you meet it |
|---|---|---|
| *טֶרֶם* | not yet | news Hebrew: *טֶרֶם הִתְקַבְּלָה הַחְלָטָה* |
| *בְּטֶרֶם* | before | *בְּטֶרֶם עֵת*, prematurely |
| *לְמַעַן* | for the sake of | *לְמַעַן הַסֵּדֶר הַטּוֹב*, for good order |
| *כִּי אִם* | but rather | formal writing, after a negative |
| *אַף כִּי* | even though | formal writing only |
| *פֶּן* | lest | formal writing only |
| *הִנֵּה* | behold, here is | fully alive: *הִנֵּה הוּא בָּא* |
| *כֹּה* | thus, so | fully alive: *עַד כֹּה*, so far |
| *אָנוֹכִי* | I | quotation only |
| *זוּלַת* | apart from, other than | formal writing |
| *אֲשֶׁר* | that, which | formal writing, and chapter 8 |

*טֶרֶם* is the one to take seriously, because it is not archaic at all in
writing: *הַוַּועֲדָה טֶרֶם סִיְּימָה אֶת עֲבוֹדָתָהּ* is ordinary news
Hebrew for "the committee has not yet finished its work". Note that the verb
after it is past tense and the meaning is a present perfect negative.

## The infinitive absolute

Biblical Hebrew could double a verb for emphasis, using a bare form with no
person on it at all. The construction is dead, and several of its outputs are
not.

::: examples
מוֹת יָמוּת || he shall surely die
שָׁמוֹעַ תִּשְׁמְעוּ || if you truly listen
הָלוֹךְ וָשׁוֹב || there and back
הַשְׁכֵּם וְהַעֲרֵב || morning and night, constantly
:::

*הָלוֹךְ וָשׁוֹב* is the live one, and it is not literary: it is what is
printed on a return ticket. *כַּרְטִיס הָלוֹךְ וָשׁוֹב* is what you ask for
at the station.

Its descendant is very much alive in written Hebrew: *הוֹלֵךְ וְ* plus an
adjective or a verb means "steadily more so".

::: examples
הַמַּצָּב הוֹלֵךְ וּמַחְמִיר. || the situation is steadily getting worse
הַמִּסְפָּרִים הוֹלְכִים וְגוֹדְלִים. || the numbers keep growing
:::

## The stock of phrases

This is the part that pays. Biblical phrases are not decoration in Israeli
speech, they are ordinary vocabulary, and several of them have drifted a long
way from what the verse meant.

| Phrase | Literally | What it means now |
|---|---|---|
| עַיִן תַּחַת עַיִן | an eye for an eye | exact retaliation, tit for tat |
| אוֹר לַגּוֹיִים | a light to the nations | Israel as a moral example, often said drily |
| מִלְחֶמֶת מִצְוָוה | an obligatory war | a fight there was no choice about |
| בְּצֶלֶם אֱלוֹהִים | in the image of God | basic human dignity; treat people properly |
| קוֹל קוֹרֵא בַּמִּדְבָּר | a voice calling in the desert | a warning nobody is listening to |
| חֶרֶב פִּיפִיּוֹת | a double-mouthed sword | a double-edged sword, it cuts both ways |
| עַם קְשֵׁה עוֹרֶף | a stiff-necked people | stubborn, said by Israelis about Israelis |
| אֵין חָדָשׁ תַּחַת הַשֶּׁמֶשׁ | nothing new under the sun | it has all happened before |
| אֶרֶץ זָבַת חָלָב וּדְבַשׁ | a land flowing with milk and honey | a place of plenty, usually ironic |
| הֲשׁוֹמֵר אָחִי אָנוֹכִי | am I my brother's keeper | not my problem, said to shame someone |
| כְּאִישׁ אֶחָד בְּלֵב אֶחָד | as one man with one heart | total unanimity |
| בְּזֵיעַת אַפֶּיךָ | by the sweat of your brow | the hard way, by real work |
| אֶבֶן נֶגֶף | a stone of stumbling | an obstacle, a sticking point |
| כְּחוֹמֶר בְּיַד הַיּוֹצֵר | like clay in the potter's hand | entirely at someone's mercy |
| נְבִיא בְּעִירוֹ | a prophet in his own town | nobody is respected at home |
| הַכּוֹל הֶבֶל | it is all vapour | it is all pointless |

::: examples
הַהֶסְכֵּם הַזֶּה הוּא חֶרֶב פִּיפִיּוֹת. || that agreement is a double-edged sword
הִזְהַרְתִּי אוֹתָם, אֲבָל הָיִיתִי קוֹל קוֹרֵא בַּמִּדְבָּר. || I warned them, but I was a voice in the wilderness
הַתַּקְצִיב הוּא אֶבֶן נֶגֶף בַּמַּשָּׂא וּמַתָּן. || the budget is a sticking point in the negotiations
הִצְבִּיעוּ כְּאִישׁ אֶחָד בְּלֵב אֶחָד. || they voted with complete unanimity
:::

::: rule
Treat a Biblical phrase as a single vocabulary item. Learn what it means now,
not what it meant then, and do not try to take it apart grammatically.
:::

## What is safe to skip

All of the morphology. You do not need to conjugate a Biblical verb, you do
not need the jussive, you do not need to know why *וַיֹּאמֶר* has a dagesh in
the yod. Nobody will ever ask you to produce one, and a modern text will never
require you to build one.

What you do need is recognition: that *וַ* plus a future-looking verb is a
past narrative, that *טֶרֶם* means not yet, and that a phrase you cannot parse
may be three thousand years old and mean something perfectly ordinary. Look it
up as a phrase, not as grammar.

::: warning
Using these in your own speech is a judgement call. *עַד כֹּה*, *טֶרֶם* and
*הִנֵּה* are safe in writing. Dropping *הֲשׁוֹמֵר אָחִי אָנוֹכִי* into a
conversation is a strong move and will get a reaction, which may be the one
you wanted or may not.
:::

::: exercise Reading the old layer
What does *וַיֹּאמֶר* mean? || and he said || vav plus a future-looking form is a past narrative
What does *וַיֵּלֶךְ* mean? || and he went || the same construction on *לָלֶכֶת*
What does *טֶרֶם הִתְקַבְּלָה הַחְלָטָה* mean? || no decision has yet been taken || *טֶרֶם* is live in news Hebrew, not archaic
Where would you see *הָלוֹךְ וָשׁוֹב* written? || on a return ticket || an infinitive absolute doing an everyday job
What does *הַמַּצָּב הוֹלֵךְ וּמַחְמִיר* mean? || the situation is steadily getting worse || *הוֹלֵךְ וְ* plus a verb means increasingly
What does *חֶרֶב פִּיפִיּוֹת* mean? || a double-edged sword, something that cuts both ways || literally a sword with two mouths
What does *אֶבֶן נֶגֶף* mean? || an obstacle, a sticking point || common in news and in argument
Do you need to conjugate Biblical verbs? || no || recognise them, treat the phrases as vocabulary
:::

# Object endings on verbs

You already know how to say "I saw him": *רָאִיתִי אוֹתוֹ*, the verb followed
by *אֶת* carrying a pronoun ending. That is what everybody says, and it is
what you should keep saying. But Hebrew has an older way of doing the same
job, which folds the object into the verb itself, and it never died. It is
alive in law, in literature, in liturgy, in the Bible and in a certain kind of
newspaper sentence.

::: rule
This is a reading skill and nothing else. Learn to recognise *רְאִיתִיו*.
Do not produce it. A learner who says *רְאִיתִיו* out loud sounds like a
nineteenth-century novel that has started walking around.
:::

## What is happening

The object pronoun that *אֶת* was carrying gets attached to the end of the
verb instead, and the verb's vowels shrink to make room.

::: examples
רָאִיתִי אוֹתוֹ becomes רְאִיתִיו || I saw him
שָׁמַרְתִּי אוֹתָם becomes שְׁמַרְתִּים || I kept them
הוּא שָׁמַר אוֹתוֹ becomes שְׁמָרוֹ || he kept it
מָצָאתִי אוֹתָהּ becomes מְצָאתִיהָ || I found her
:::

The meaning does not change at all. Nothing is added and nothing is lost: it
is the same sentence in a higher register, the way "whom did you see" is the
same question as "who did you see".

## The endings on a past-tense verb

Here is *שָׁמַר* /shamar/, to keep or guard, in the third person masculine
singular and in the first person singular, with each object hung off the end.

| Object | *אֶת* + pronoun | on *שָׁמַר* | on *שָׁמַרְתִּי* |
|---|---|---|---|
| me | אוֹתִי | שְׁמָרַנִי | |
| you (m) | אוֹתְךָ | שְׁמָרְךָ | שְׁמַרְתִּיךָ |
| you (f) | אוֹתָךְ | שְׁמָרֵךְ | שְׁמַרְתִּיךְ |
| him, it | אוֹתוֹ | שְׁמָרוֹ | שְׁמַרְתִּיו |
| her | אוֹתָהּ | שְׁמָרָהּ | שְׁמַרְתִּיהָ |
| us | אוֹתָנוּ | שְׁמָרָנוּ | |
| them (m) | אוֹתָם | שְׁמָרָם | שְׁמַרְתִּים |
| them (f) | אוֹתָן | שְׁמָרָן | |

Three cells are empty. Two of them cannot exist: a verb meaning "I guarded"
cannot take "me" or "us" as its object. The third is a gap in practice rather
than in principle: the feminine plural object is vanishingly rare in this
construction, and a text that needs it reaches for the masculine.

::: note
Notice the vowels. *שָׁמַר* with a kamatz becomes *שְׁמָרוֹ* with a shva: the
stress has moved onto the ending, and the first vowel has thinned out. This is
the same reduction you saw in *כָּתַב* becoming *כָּתְבָה*, applied again.
:::

## The endings on an infinitive

This is the form you will actually meet, because it survives in modern legal
and journalistic Hebrew, where the other forms do not.

| Infinitive | With an object | |
|---|---|---|
| לִרְאוֹת | לִרְאוֹתוֹ | to see him |
| לִרְאוֹת | לִרְאוֹתָהּ | to see her |
| לְהַכִּיר | לְהַכִּירָהּ | to know her, to meet her |
| לְשַׁחְרֵר | לְשַׁחְרְרוֹ | to release him |
| לְפַטֵּר | לְפַטְּרוֹ | to dismiss him |
| לְהַזְמִין | לְהַזְמִינָם | to invite them |

Court reporting runs on these. *בַּקָּשָׁה לְעוֹצְרוֹ*, a request to detain
him, *הַחְלָטָה לְפַטְּרוֹ*, a decision to dismiss him. The alternative,
*בַּקָּשָׁה לַעֲצוֹר אוֹתוֹ*, is perfectly correct Hebrew and is simply not
the house style of that genre.

## Four real sentences

::: examples
בֵּית הַמִּשְׁפָּט הוֹרָה לְשַׁחְרְרוֹ לְמַעֲצַר בַּיִת. || the court ordered him released to house arrest
הַוַּועֲדָה הֶחְלִיטָה לְזַמְּנוֹ לְעֵדוּת. || the committee decided to summon him to testify
רְאִיתִיו עוֹמֵד בַּשַּׁעַר, וְלֹא הִכַּרְתִּיו. || I saw him standing at the gate, and did not recognise him
יְבָרֶכְךָ ה' וְיִשְׁמְרֶךָ. || may the Lord bless you and keep you
:::

Unpick them one at a time. *לְשַׁחְרְרוֹ* is *לְשַׁחְרֵר* plus *וֹ*, "him":
the tzere in the last syllable thins to a shva when the ending is added.
*לְזַמְּנוֹ* is *לְזַמֵּן* plus the same *וֹ*. *הִכַּרְתִּיו* is
*הִכַּרְתִּי* plus *ו*, and the *תִּי* is still visible in the middle, which
is the clue: when you see *תִּיו*, *תִּים* or *תִּיהָ* at the end of a word,
cut it off and what remains is an ordinary first person past tense.
*יְבָרֶכְךָ* is the future *יְבָרֵךְ* plus *ךָ*, "you", from the priestly
blessing in Numbers, which is the most-heard sentence in this chapter.

::: tip
The recognition trick is mechanical. Chop off *וֹ*, *הָ*, *ם*, *ן*, *ךָ*,
*נִי* or *נוּ* from the end of an unfamiliar verb and see whether a verb you
know is left. If it is, the piece you chopped off is the object.
:::

## The same endings on prepositions, which you do use

This is the contrast that matters, because the endings are the same set and
one half of the system is everyday while the other half is museum Hebrew.

::: examples
דִּיבַּרְתִּי אִיתּוֹ עָלֶיהָ. || I spoke with him about her
כָּתוּב בּוֹ שֶׁהַמִּשְׂרָד סָגוּר. || it says in it that the office is closed
קִיבַּלְתִּי מִמֶּנּוּ מִכְתָּב. || I got a letter from him
פָּנִיתִי אֵלֶיהָ וְלֹא עָנְתָה. || I turned to her and she did not answer
:::

Nobody would ever replace *עָלֶיהָ* or *מִמֶּנּוּ* with anything; they are
the only forms there are. A preposition in Hebrew must take its pronoun as a
suffix. A verb may, and in speech never does.

::: warning
*אָמַר לוֹ* stays *אָמַר לוֹ*. It looks as though it should compress, and it
cannot, because *לוֹ* is not the verb's object: it is the preposition *לְ*
with an ending. Compressing it would produce *אֲמָרוֹ*, which means "he said
it", a different sentence. The test is whether the pronoun was riding on
*אֶת*. Only an *אֶת* pronoun can climb onto the verb.
:::

::: note
The *וֹ* on *שְׁמָרוֹ* and the *וֹ* on *בּוֹ* and the *וֹ* on *סִפְרוֹ* are
the same historical ending doing three jobs: object of a verb, object of a
preposition, possessor of a noun. Hebrew has one set of pronoun suffixes and
hangs it on whatever will take it.
:::

::: exercise Unfold the verb
Rewrite *רְאִיתִיו* the ordinary way || *רָאִיתִי אוֹתוֹ* || I saw him
Rewrite *שְׁמַרְתִּים* the ordinary way || *שָׁמַרְתִּי אוֹתָם* || the *ם* is "them"
What does *לְהַכִּירָהּ* mean? || to know her, to get to know her || infinitive plus the ending for "her"
What does *לְפַטְּרוֹ* mean, and where would you read it? || to dismiss him, in a news report or a legal document || never in speech
Why can *אָמַר לוֹ* not become one word? || *לוֹ* is a preposition with an ending, not the verb's object || only an *אֶת* pronoun can attach to the verb
Is *עָלָיו* formal? || no, it is the everyday and only form || prepositions always take the suffix
Should you use *רְאִיתִיו* when speaking? || no || it is a reading skill; producing it sounds strange
What is left when you chop *תִּיהָ* off *מְצָאתִיהָ*? || *מָצָאתִי*, I found || and the *הָ* is the object, her
:::
